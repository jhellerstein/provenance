\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Algebraic provenance is one of the major success stories of database theory.
By representing query results as elements of a commutative semiring—most famously
as provenance polynomials—researchers have obtained a uniform account of
\emph{why}- and \emph{how}-provenance, explanations, uncertainty propagation, and
query optimization across a wide range of query languages
\cite{green2007provenance,suciu2011probabilistic}.
This algebraic view has proved remarkably robust: once query semantics are
single-valued and monotone, provenance reduces to arithmetic.

Yet this success rests on a strong and often implicit assumption:
that query semantics are already \emph{resolved}.
That is, for each input history or database instance, the specification admits a
single semantic outcome (or a single equivalence class of outcomes).
In practice, many of the most important semantic settings in databases and
distributed systems violate this assumption.

Modern systems routinely operate in settings that are intrinsically
\emph{ambiguous}.
Negation in logic programming admits multiple models.
Snapshot isolation admits multiple concurrent futures.
Distributed coordination and failure semantics admit multiple consistent
interpretations.
Probabilistic databases and Monte Carlo methods explicitly reason over sets of
possible worlds.
In all of these cases, the semantic object of interest is not a single outcome,
but a \emph{set} of admissible outcomes.
Algebraic provenance, in its classical form, has little to say in such spaces.

\medskip

\paragraph{Thesis.}
This paper argues that the gap between ambiguous semantics and algebraic
provenance is not accidental.
Rather, provenance succeeds precisely because it operates \emph{after} a class
of semantic commitments has been made.
These commitments—about negation, ordering, visibility, membership, or
consistency—are what collapse a multi-valued specification into a resolved one.
We call such a collection of commitments an \emph{interpretation}.

We build on the framework of \emph{interpretation complexity} introduced
in~\cite{hellerstein2026interpretation}, which models interpretation as a semantic
refinement process with measurable structure.
An interpretation induces a \emph{quotient} on the space of admissible outcomes,
collapsing ambiguity into a resolved semantic space.
This paper shows that algebraic provenance applies precisely in these induced
quotient spaces.

\medskip

\paragraph{Interpretation as quotient engineering.}
Viewed through this lens, provenance is not a primitive feature of query
semantics, but a consequence of \emph{quotient engineering}.
Different choices of interpretation induce different quotients:
some collapse ambiguity cheaply, others require deep chains of dependent
commitments.
We characterize interpretations along two independent axes:
\emph{cost}, measuring how many semantic commitments are introduced, and
\emph{depth}, measuring their irreducible dependency structure.
Cost captures the \emph{volume} of commitment; depth captures its
\emph{semantic entanglement}.

This distinction is crucial.
Two interpretations may have the same cost but radically different depth,
corresponding to whether commitments commute or must be staged.
Depth, rather than cost, determines when resolution can be parallelized,
incrementalized, or avoided entirely.

\medskip

\paragraph{Design space of quotients.}
A key contribution of this paper is to make explicit a small but expressive
\emph{design space of quotients}.
In Datalog with negation, this space includes stratified, well-founded, and stable
semantics.
In probabilistic databases, it includes independence assumptions and factorized
world models.
Each choice corresponds to a different interpretation, with different cost/depth
profiles and different algebraic consequences.
Classical semiring provenance emerges only after sufficient resolution has been
performed.

\medskip

\paragraph{Scope and roadmap.}
Rather than attempting to cover all sources of semantic ambiguity, this paper
uses Datalog with negation and probabilistic databases as a focused sandbox.
These settings are familiar to PODS readers, yet rich enough to exhibit the
phenomena of interest.
Our goal is not to propose a new semantics for negation or probability, but to
clarify the algebraic consequences of existing ones.

The paper proceeds as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:background} recalls the interpretation framework and the notions
of cost and depth.
Section~\ref{sec:quotients} formalizes interpretation as quotient engineering.
Section~\ref{sec:negation} analyzes stratified, well-founded, and stable negation
through this lens.
Section~\ref{sec:probabilistic} shows how classical semiring provenance is
recovered after resolution in probabilistic databases.
We conclude by discussing broader implications for provenance, concurrency, and
distributed systems.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A formal account of \emph{interpretation as quotient engineering} for
        ambiguous semantics.
  \item Structural measures of semantic commitment: \emph{cost} (how many
        commitments) and \emph{depth} (their dependency structure).
  \item An explicit \emph{design space of quotients}, encompassing stratified,
        well-founded, and stable interpretations.
  \item Worked examples showing where and why algebraic (semiring) provenance
        applies only \emph{after} resolution.
\end{itemize}

\section{Overview: From Ambiguity to Algebra via Quotients}
\label{sec:overview}

We begin by informally introducing the central objects of the paper and the
conceptual move that connects them.
The goal of this section is not technical precision, but orientation: to make
clear what problem we are solving and why algebraic provenance naturally appears
\emph{after} semantic resolution rather than before.

Our analysis builds directly on a prior complexity framework introduced in an
earlier paper~\cite{interpretation-complexity-arxiv}, which formalizes
\emph{interpretation cost} and \emph{interpretation depth} as measures of the
semantic commitments required to resolve ambiguity.
That work develops the complexity theory in full and studies its consequences
across several computational models.
In this paper, we treat that framework as given and focus instead on its
\emph{semantic payoff}: how different interpretations engineer quotients that
enable algebraic provenance.

\subsection{Ambiguous specifications}

Throughout the paper, we model semantics as a (possibly multi-valued)
specification
\[
  \Spec : \Hist \to 2^O ,
\]
mapping an input history or instance $H$ to a set of admissible outcomes.
Ambiguity is represented directly: $\Spec(H)$ may contain many distinct outcomes,
corresponding to different consistent interpretations of the same evidence.

This formulation subsumes a wide range of familiar settings:
Datalog programs with negation admit multiple models;
snapshot isolation admits multiple concurrent futures;
probabilistic databases admit multiple possible worlds.
In all of these cases, ambiguity is not an artifact of incomplete information,
but a fundamental property of the semantics.

When $\Spec(H)$ is single-valued for all $H$, we say the specification is
\emph{resolved}.
Classical algebraic provenance lives almost exclusively in this regime.

\subsection{Interpretations as semantic commitments}

An \emph{interpretation} is a structured collection of semantic commitments that
restrict the admissible outcomes of a specification.
Formally, an interpretation acts by refining $\Spec$ to a new specification
$\Spec \mid I$, obtained by eliminating outcomes that are incompatible with the
commitments in $I$.

Crucially, interpretations are not arbitrary filters.
They are built from \emph{interpretive predicates} that may apply conditionally,
depending on which outcomes remain admissible.
As a result, different predicates may or may not commute:
some commitments can be made independently, while others must be staged.

This structure gives rise to two independent measures:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Cost}, measuring how many semantic commitments are introduced, and
  \item \emph{Depth}, measuring the irreducible dependency structure among those
        commitments.
\end{itemize}

Cost captures the volume of commitment; depth captures its semantic entanglement.
Depth, rather than cost, determines whether commitments can be parallelized or
must be introduced sequentially.

\subsection{Resolution induces a quotient}

Applying an interpretation $I$ induces a quotient on the outcome space.
Outcomes that differ only in ways ruled out by $I$ are identified, while outcomes
that violate the commitments are eliminated entirely.
When $\Spec \mid I$ is resolved, this quotient collapses ambiguity to a single
semantic outcome (or equivalence class).

This perspective reframes semantic resolution as \emph{quotient engineering}.
Different interpretations induce different quotients:
some coarse, some fine-grained; some cheap, some deep.
The space of possible interpretations thus defines a design space of quotients,
each with distinct semantic and algebraic consequences.

\subsection{Why algebra appears after resolution}

Algebraic provenance relies on two properties:
single-valued semantics and monotonic reasoning.
Both emerge naturally after sufficient resolution has occurred.

Once $\Spec \mid I$ is resolved, provenance can be computed over the induced
quotient space.
In this space, alternative derivations combine algebraically, explanations
compose, and uncertainty propagates via arithmetic.
Before resolution, however, provenance expressions mix fundamentally incompatible
semantic possibilities.

Seen this way, algebraic provenance is not a competing semantic theory, but a
downstream beneficiary of interpretation.
Interpretations determine \emph{when} and \emph{where} algebra applies.

\subsection{A minimal running example}

As a running intuition, consider a Datalog rule with negation:
\[
  p \leftarrow \neg q .
\]
Before any commitment is made about $q$, the specification admits outcomes in
which $p$ is either true or false.
Applying a negation-as-failure commitment eliminates outcomes where $q$ holds,
resolving the ambiguity.
Only after this resolution does the truth of $p$ become fixed and algebraic
reasoning apply.

Later sections develop this intuition formally and show how different choices of
interpretation—stratified, well-founded, or stable—induce different quotients
with different cost and depth profiles.

\subsection{Looking ahead}

The remainder of the paper makes this picture precise.
We define interpretations, cost, and depth formally; characterize admissible
quotients; and show how classical provenance is recovered in resolved spaces.
Throughout, the guiding principle is that ambiguity is not an obstacle to
algebra, but a signal that interpretation has not yet been accounted for.
