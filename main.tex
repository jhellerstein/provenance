\begin{abstract}
Algebraic provenance explains query results by tracing derivations under a fixed,
resolved semantics.
Many systems, however, operate under \emph{semantic ambiguity}:
logic programs with negation admit multiple accepted models, transactional
systems admit multiple consistent executions, and distributed or policy-aware
systems admit multiple outcomes compatible with the same history.
In such settings, it is not enough to explain \emph{what follows;} one must also
explain \emph{under which semantic resolution it follows}.

This paper introduces \emph{interpretation provenance}, a framework for making
semantic resolution explicit and explainable.
An \emph{interpretation} is a structured collection of semantic commitments that
resolves ambiguity by refining admissible outcomes.
We show that provenance becomes meaningful only \emph{after} such resolution.
% , and
% that classical algebraic provenance arises as a special case when semantics are
% already resolved.
We formalize how interpretations structure provenance prior to resolution, giving
rise to a layered, dependency-sensitive form that becomes ordinary semiring
provenance once a resolving interpretation is fixed.
We introduce \emph{layer certificates} as sufficient summaries of semantic
commitments for downstream reasoning.

The work extends the reach of formal data provenance to a range of 
settings where ambiguity is intrinsic.
To illustrate, we instantiate the framework in two domains with fundamentally different sources
of ambiguity: negation in Datalog and scheduling in transactional systems.
Across both settings, interpretation provenance clarifies which semantic
commitments were required to justify results, and why interpretations
are required to complement classical provenance in the presence of ambiguity.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Algebraic provenance has given database theory a powerful and durable language for
explaining query results.
Declarative query semantics provide a well-defined outcome,
and provenance provides clean, uniform explanations of query
results—supporting why- and how-provenance, uncertainty propagation,
and optimization through a common algebraic framework
\cite{green2007provenance,suciu2011probabilistic}.
In these settings, explanation reduces to arithmetic, and this reduction has been
remarkably effective.

Practical data systems can build partly on this foundation, but they also operate in a
broader semantic landscape.
Transactional workloads admit multiple serializable executions.
Logic programs with negation admit multiple distinct models.
Distributed systems can yield multiple admissible orderings consistent with the same
history.
In such settings, a history determines not a single outcome, but a structured
space of multiple admissible outcomes.

Natural provenance questions often require more involved answers in this context
than classical provenance can provide.
For example, the reason that a tuple was
returned by a query may depend on which serializable execution was realized by a scheduler.
New questions arise in this context as well that have no analog in classical provenance.
Was this tuple returned due to a particular choice of isolation level---e.g., snapshot isolation versus serializability?
Did this tuple appear because of an inevitable consequence of the data, or because of a particular scheduling choice made by the system?
These familiar transactional examples are emblematic 
of the gap between classical data provenance and a range of systems research on 
lineage~\cite{sigelman2010dapper,seltzer2005provenance,bates2015trustworthy}
that has lacked a formal foundation.
Answering such questions requires explaining not only how results were derived,
but \emph{how semantic ambiguity was resolved}.

In prior work~\cite{hellerstein2026interpretation}, we introduced
\emph{interpretations} as formal objects that resolve semantic ambiguity by
introducing structured semantic commitments.
That work develops a complexity theory of interpretation, characterizing the
\emph{cost} and \emph{depth} of semantic resolution.
Interpretations explain both semantic ambiguity arising within formal languages
(as in logic programming with negation) and ambiguity arising from execution
environments (as in transactional and distributed systems).

This paper shows how interpretations give rise to a new form of provenance.
We introduce \emph{interpretation provenance}, which records the semantic
commitments under which results become well defined.
Once semantic ambiguity has been resolved by an interpretation, classical data
provenance applies directly, providing the derivational structure of results
within that interpretation.

We illustrate interpretation provenance in two domains with fundamentally different
sources of semantic ambiguity.
Logic programming with negation provides the canonical, mathematically explicit
instance of semantic ambiguity and resolution in computer science, serving as a
control experiment for interpretation provenance.
We then turn to transactional systems, where ambiguity arises from concurrency and
isolation choices, and show how interpretation provenance provides a fuller
accounting of query results than classical approaches. In the appendix we also examine
examples from distributed systems observability, a widely-used setting for system
lineage~\cite{sigelman2010dapper}.

\begin{example}[Provenance under transactional ambiguity]
  \label{ex:example}
Consider a database with a binary relation $S$ 
and an initially empty instance (EDB).
The following set of transactions is issued concurrently:
\[
\begin{array}{ll}
T_1: \textsf{insert } S(1,b) \ (x_1) \qquad &
T_2: \textsf{insert } S(2,b) \ (x_2)\\
T_3: \textsf{insert } S(3,c) \ (x_3) \qquad &
T_4: \textsf{insert } S(4,d) \ (x_4)\\
T_5: \textsf{delete } S(4,d). &
\end{array}
\]
Subsequently, a query transaction $T_Q$ evaluates $Q(y) \drule S(k,y)$.

Under serializability, the observed history admits multiple executions.
Tuples $\{(b), (c)\}$ appear in $Q$ for every serializable execution.
Tuple $(d)$ is contingent: it appears in some executions and not others, depending
on whether the delete is ordered before or after the query. Before resolving this
ambiguity, there is no single classical provenance question for $d$. Indeed,
the family of provenance itself depends on how the ambiguity is resolved: in 
one order the question is \emph{why} $d$ appears, while in another it is \emph{why-not} $d$.
Interpretation provenance makes this dependence explicit.
It records the semantic commitments under which the query result is interpreted,
together with the ordinary derivational explanation that applies once those
commitments are fixed.
Section~\ref{sec:concurrency} formalizes this example by writing down the corresponding
resolving interpretations and provenance expressions, and the example is 
worked in full in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-concurrency}.
\end{example}

\paragraph{Contributions.}
This paper (i) introduces \emph{interpretation provenance} as a foundation for explaining
results under ambiguous semantics; (ii) shows how resolving interpretations recover classical
algebraic provenance as a special case; (iii) formalizes certificates as a minimal interface
for recording semantic commitments; (iv) applies the framework to transactional systems to
explain concurrency-dependent query results; and (v) identifies robustness across
interpretations as a new class of provenance questions.

\section{Preliminaries: Ambiguous Semantics and Refinement}
\label{sec:prelim}

This section recalls the core semantic constructs from our earlier work on
interpretation complexity~\cite{hellerstein2026interpretation}, adapted here as
background.
We present them in a streamlined form, emphasizing semantic ambiguity,
conditioning, and dependency, in order to characterize what it means for a
specification to admit multiple admissible meanings and the semantic structure
that such ambiguity already entails.
We intentionally defer complexity measures, algebraic structure, and any
discussion of how interpretations are chosen, optimized, or justified to later
sections.

\subsection{Histories}

We model partial information using partially-ordered histories.
Although this terminology is sometimes associated with Lamport-style
happens-before relations, we do not assume communicating sequential processes
or any other specific execution model.
We simply record any known precedence constraints among events.

\begin{definition}[History]
A \emph{history} is a finite partially ordered set
\[
  H = (E, \rightarrow),
\]
where $E$ is a set of events and $\rightarrow \subseteq E \times E$ is a strict
partial order representing causal precedence.
\end{definition}

The order $\rightarrow$ expresses only irreducible precedence constraints: if
$e \rightarrow e'$, then $e$ must be known to have occurred before $e'$.
No other ordering is assumed.
In particular, histories do \emph{not} impose a total order, a notion of time, or
an execution schedule.

We write $\Hist$ for the class of histories under consideration.
This choice is parametric and deliberately abstract: histories may represent
database updates, transaction invocations, message deliveries, or any other
events that constrain semantic interpretation.

\begin{definition}[History Extension]
For histories $H_1=(E_1,\rightarrow_1)$ and $H_2=(E_2,\rightarrow_2)$, we write
$H_1 \sqsubseteq H_2$ if:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $E_1 \subseteq E_2$, and
  \item $\rightarrow_1 = \rightarrow_2 \cap (E_1 \times E_1)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

Intuitively, histories grow by appending new events and new precedence constraints,
without revising the ordering of previously observed events.
We interpret $\sqsubseteq$ as information growth, not as time or execution.

\subsection{Outcomes and Specifications}

\begin{definition}[Outcome]
An \emph{outcome} is an element of a set $O$ of fully specified semantic objects.
\end{definition}

Outcomes represent what an observer may conclude once all semantic choices have
been fixed.
Examples include a database state, a serial execution of transactions, a model of
a logic program, the visible state of an automaton, or a completed task result.

\begin{definition}[Specification]
A \emph{specification} is a function
\[
  \Spec : \Hist \to 2^{O},
\]
mapping each history $H \in \Hist$ to a (possibly empty) set of admissible outcomes.
\end{definition}

The set $\Spec(H)$ captures all outcomes consistent with the evidence in $H$.
When $|\Spec(H)| > 1$, the specification admits multiple mutually incompatible
meanings.
When $|\Spec(H)| = 1$, the meaning of $H$ is fully determined.

Ambiguity in this sense is common and intentional.
Specifications for Datalog with negation, transactional histories, and distributed
executions all allow multiple admissible outcomes for the same history.
In such settings, semantic questions cannot be posed until ambiguity is constrained
by interpretation.\footnote{%
This notion of ambiguity—multiplicity in $\Spec(H) \subseteq O$—is distinct from
ambiguity represented \emph{within} an outcome domain $O$ itself (e.g., probabilistic
or multi-valued outcomes).
Our framework accommodates both, but interpretation provenance is concerned with
resolving multiplicity at the level of admissible outcomes.
We discuss this distinction in more detail in Section~\ref{sec:negation} and
Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-datalog}.}


\subsection{Semantic Refinement}

Ambiguity is constrained by making additional semantic commitments.
We model these commitments as \emph{interpretive predicates}, understood as
semantic conditions that \emph{filter} the set of admissible outcomes while
preserving feasibility.

\begin{definition}[Interpretive Predicate (as Outcome Filter)]
An \emph{interpretive predicate} is a function
\[
  \varphi : \Hist \times 2^{O} \to 2^{O}
\]
such that for all histories $H$ and outcome sets $S \subseteq O$:
(\emph{Shrinkage}) $\varphi(H,S) \subseteq S$, and (\emph{Feasibility}) if $S \neq \emptyset$ then $\varphi(H,S) \neq \emptyset$.
\end{definition}

Interpretive predicates correspond to the refinement operators studied in our
earlier work~\cite{hellerstein2026interpretation}: each predicate induces a
monotone, shrinking transformation on the set of admissible outcomes.
In this paper, we adopt the predicate view, which better supports provenance
explanations and dependency analysis.
An interpretive predicate restricts admissible outcomes without introducing new ones
or rendering the specification infeasible.
Interpretive predicates may be \emph{ambiguity-sensitive}: their effect may depend on the entire
admissible set $S$, not merely on individual outcomes.

\begin{definition}[Refinement by Conditioning]
Given a specification $\Spec$ and an interpretive predicate $\varphi$, the
\emph{refinement} of $\Spec$ by $\varphi$ is the specification
\[
  (\Spec \mid \varphi)(H) \triangleq \varphi(H,\Spec(H)).
\]
\end{definition}

Refinement acts pointwise on histories but may depend on the current set of
admissible outcomes.
Sequential refinement corresponds to conditioning on multiple semantic
commitments.

\subsection{Interpretations}

Individual interpretive predicates typically express only partial semantic
commitments.
We therefore model semantic resolution using finite conjunctions of refinement
predicates, interpreted as sequential conditioning.

\begin{definition}[Interpretation]
An \emph{interpretation} is a finite conjunction
\[
  I = \varphi_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \varphi_m
\]
of interpretive predicates, where $\wedge$ denotes sequential refinement.
Throughout the paper, interpretation provenance is expressed by conditioning
semantic objects on an interpretation; no algebraic structure is imposed prior to conditioning.
\end{definition}

Refinement by an interpretation is defined by sequential application:
\[
  \Spec \mid I
  \;\triangleq\;
  (\cdots((\Spec \mid \varphi_1)\mid \varphi_2)\cdots)\mid \varphi_m .
\]

Because interpretive predicates may be ambiguity-sensitive, the order of conjuncts
can matter.
This non-commutativity is the source of semantic dependency studied in later
sections.

\begin{definition}[Resolved Specification and Resolving Interpretation]
A specification $\Spec$ is \emph{resolved} if for every history $H$,
$|\Spec(H)| = 1$.
An interpretation $I$ \emph{resolves} $\Spec$ if $\Spec \mid I$ is resolved.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Restriction and Identification}

Fix a specification $\Spec$ and an interpretation $I$.
For each history $H$, refinement by $I$ produces a restricted set of admissible
outcomes $(\Spec \mid I)(H)$.

The semantic effect of interpretation has two aspects:
\emph{restriction}, in which outcomes incompatible with the commitments in $I$ are eliminated and play no further semantic role, and \emph{identification}, in which some of the remaining admissible outcomes become semantically indistinguishable.

Formally, for a history $H$ write
\[
  O_H^I \triangleq (\Spec \mid I)(H).
\]
We say that two outcomes $o,o' \in O_H^I$ are \emph{equivalent under $I$ at $H$},
written $o \sim_{I,H} o'$, if no interpretive predicate appearing in $I$
distinguishes between them at $H$.

\begin{definition}[Interpretation-Induced Quotient]
For each history $H$, interpretation $I$ induces a quotient
\[
  O_H^I \big/ {\sim_{I,H}},
\]
obtained by identifying admissible outcomes that are equivalent under $I$.
\end{definition}

The equivalence relation $\sim_{I,H}$ is total on the restricted domain $O_H^I$.
Outcomes ruled out by the interpretation are simply absent from the quotient.

\begin{remark}[History-indexed quotients]
Because interpretive predicates may depend on the history and on intermediate
admissible sets, equivalence under an interpretation is defined relative to a
history.
Interpretations therefore induce a family of quotients indexed by histories,
rather than a single global quotient on $O$.
\end{remark}

These notions of refinement, restriction, and identification provide the raw
semantic substrate on which we build algebraic structure in the next section.

\section{Interpretations and Provenance Structure}
\label{sec:conditional-semiring}
This section connects the semantic notion of interpretation
(Section~\ref{sec:prelim}) to provenance.
Fix a specification $\Spec$ and a resolving interpretation $I$.
Interpretation provenance records both the semantic commitments fixed by $I$ and
the derivational provenance that applies after conditioning on $I$.

\subsection{Syntax of Interpretation Provenance}

We now introduce a compact notation for \emph{interpretation provenance}.
This subsection introduces no new semantic machinery.
It packages together notions already defined—interpretations, resolution, and
classical provenance—into a single explanatory object.
Throughout the paper, interpretation provenance is expressed by conditioning semantic objects on an interpretation; no algebraic structure is imposed prior to conditioning.

\paragraph{Interpretation provenance values.}
Fix a commutative semiring $(K,\otimes,1)$ for derivational provenance
(e.g., polynomial provenance).
For a query $Q$ and tuple $t$, write $\Prov_{Q \mid I}(t) \in K$ for the
ordinary (depth--0) provenance computed under the conditioned specification
$\Spec \mid I$.
We define \emph{interpretation provenance} as the pair
\[
  \mathsf{IP}_Q(t) \;\triangleq\; (I,\, \Prov_{Q \mid I}(t)).
\]

When convenient, we may write $I \cdot k$ as an abbreviation for the pair $(I,k)$,
but all semantics are expressed via conditioning on $I$.

The intended reading is:
\begin{quote}
\emph{$k$ explains the derivation, assuming the semantic commitments fixed by $I$.}
\end{quote}

\paragraph{Relationship to classical provenance.}
Once an interpretation $I$ resolves $\Spec$, semantic ambiguity is fixed for the purpose of provenance.
Query evaluation under $\Spec \mid I$ is single-valued, and classical semiring
provenance applies without modification.
Interpretation provenance therefore records classical provenance under
conditioning, together with the interpretation that justifies that conditioning.

In particular, for \emph{depth--0} specifications—where no nontrivial semantic
commitments are required—interpretation provenance coincides exactly with
classical semiring-based provenance.

\paragraph{Why interpretation provenance is linear.}
Each interpretation provenance value is indexed by a \emph{single} resolving
interpretation.
We do not form disjunctions over interpretations inside provenance values.
Questions about variation across interpretations—such as robustness,
invariance, or sensitivity to semantic change—are treated explicitly and
separately in Section~\ref{sec:robustness}.

This design choice is intentional.
Interpretation provenance is explanatory rather than speculative: it records
which semantic assumptions were actually relied upon, together with the
derivational explanation that follows once those assumptions are fixed.

We illustrate this syntax next in a transactional setting, where
different ordering commitments induce different interpretations and,
consequently, different provenance for the same query.

\subsection{Example Revisited: Resolving Transactional Ambiguity}
\label{sec:concurrency}

We now return to the transactional Example~\ref{ex:example}.
Recall that values $b, c$ are present in every serializable execution, while value $d$
is present only under certain execution orders.

We can now show the syntax of interpretation provenance for this example.
In a conflict serializability context, interpretive predicates correspond to ordering
commitments among transactions: $\varphi_{T_i \prec T_j}$ commits to
ordering the actions of transaction $T_i$ before the conflicting actions of $T_j$.
If we observe $\{(b), (c), (d)\}$ as the query result, then an example Interpretation is 
\[
I_{\mathsf{in}} \;\triangleq\; \varphi_{T_1 \prec T_2} \wedge \varphi_{T_2 \prec T_3} \wedge \varphi_{T_3 \prec T_4} \wedge \varphi_{T_4 \prec T_Q} \wedge \varphi_{T_Q \prec T_5}
\]
and the corresponding conditioned (depth--0) provenance is
\[
  \Prov_{Q \mid I_{\mathsf{in}}}(b)=x_1+x_2,\;
  \Prov_{Q \mid I_{\mathsf{in}}}(c)=x_3,\;
  \Prov_{Q \mid I_{\mathsf{in}}}(d)=x_4.
\]

Interpretation provenance pairs these expressions with the interpretation,
e.g., $\mathsf{IP}_Q(b) \triangleq (I_{\mathsf{in}},\, x_1+x_2)$.

In Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-concurrency} we work this example more fully, including 
the case where $d$ is absent from the query result.

\subsection{Summary}

Interpretation provenance pairs a resolving interpretation with classical
provenance computed under that interpretation.
Once an interpretation is fixed, provenance is classical by construction: it is
computed over the conditioned specification $\Spec \mid I$.

The following sections instantiate this framework in two settings with very
different sources of semantic ambiguity: negation in Datalog and scheduling in
transactional systems.

\section{Layer Certificates and Interpretation Provenance}
\label{sec:certificates}
% ================================================================

Interpretations resolve semantic ambiguity by conditioning a specification.
In this section we ask a complementary question:

\begin{quote}
\emph{What information must be retained to support further interpretation
\emph{and} provenance queries about the result?}
\end{quote}

Interpretation proceeds incrementally, but neither subsequent interpretation nor
provenance analysis requires access to the full refined specification.
Instead, it suffices to retain summaries that preserve exactly the semantic
distinctions relevant to future reasoning.
We formalize these summaries as \emph{layer certificates}.

\subsection{Certificates and Provenance Queries}

Fix a specification $\Spec$ and an interpretation
$I = \varphi_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \varphi_k$ with layering
$L_1,\ldots,L_k$.
Let
\[
  \Spec_i \;\triangleq\; \Spec \mid (\varphi_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \varphi_i),
  \qquad
  J \;\triangleq\; \varphi_{i+1} \wedge \cdots \wedge \varphi_k .
\]

\paragraph{Certificates.}
An interpretive predicate restricts admissible outcomes.
A \emph{certificate} summarizes this restriction.

\begin{definition}[Certificate]
Let $\varphi$ be an interpretive predicate.
A value $C$ with denotation $\llbracket C \rrbracket \subseteq O$ is a
\emph{certificate} for $\varphi$, written $\varphi \models C$, if for all histories
$H$ and nonempty $S \subseteq O$,
\[
  \varphi(H,S) \subseteq S \cap \llbracket C \rrbracket .
\]
\end{definition}

Certificates may be weaker than the predicates they summarize: they need only
exclude outcomes that would be excluded by interpretation, not encode the full
resolution process.

\paragraph{Provenance queries.}
Interpretation provenance is queried.

\begin{definition}[Provenance Query]
A \emph{provenance query} is a function
\[
  q : (\Spec \mid I) \to A ,
\]
where $A$ is an answer domain (e.g., Boolean, explanations, or dependency sets).
\end{definition}

Examples include robustness queries (“does $t$ hold under all interpretations in a
class?”), semantic-change queries (“does $t$ persist when replacing $I$ by $I'$?”),
and conditional why/why-not queries.

\paragraph{Sufficiency for continuation and queries.}
Certificates must preserve both future interpretation and the answers to intended
provenance queries.

\begin{definition}[Sufficient Certificate]
A certificate $C_i$ is \emph{sufficient for $(J,\mathcal{Q})$} if for all histories
$H$:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Continuation:}
  \[
    (\Spec_i \mid J)(H)
    =
    \bigl((\Spec \mid \chi_{C_i}) \mid J\bigr)(H);
  \]
  \item \emph{Query preservation:} for all $q \in \mathcal{Q}$,
  \[
    q\bigl((\Spec_i \mid J)(H)\bigr)
    =
    q\bigl(((\Spec \mid \chi_{C_i}) \mid J)(H)\bigr).
  \]
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

A \emph{layer certificate} is a certificate sufficient for all subsequent layers
and the intended family of provenance queries.

\subsection{Query Sensitivity of Certificates}

Supporting richer provenance queries strictly increases certificate requirements.

\begin{theorem}[Query Monotonicity]
If $\mathcal{Q}_1 \subseteq \mathcal{Q}_2$, then every certificate sufficient for
$(J,\mathcal{Q}_2)$ is sufficient for $(J,\mathcal{Q}_1)$.
\end{theorem}

\noindent
Thus, extending the space of provenance questions can only require retaining
additional semantic information, never less.

Concrete illustrations of this phenomenon appear in the appendices.
Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-datalog} shows how semantic-change queries for negation
semantics enlarge certificates beyond those needed for derivational provenance.
Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-concurrency} shows the analogous effect for robustness
queries under transactional ambiguity.

\subsection{Summary}

Layer certificates provide a principled interface between semantic resolution and
provenance analysis.
By parameterizing certificates by provenance query families, interpretation
provenance makes explicit which semantic distinctions must be retained—and why—
when supporting explanation, robustness, or semantic change.

\section{Sandbox I: Datalog with Negation}
\label{sec:negation}
% ================================================================

We instantiate interpretation provenance in a familiar and deeply studied
setting: Datalog with negation.
Our purpose is not to propose new semantics for negation, nor to compare their
expressive power.
Rather, we use classical semantics for Datalog with negation as a controlled
environment in which to observe how semantic ambiguity in a \emph{fixed}
specification gives rise to different structures of interpretation provenance.

Datalog with negation exhibits ambiguity even for a fixed program and a fixed
input instance.
Negated predicates introduce dependencies that delay semantic commitment: whether
an atom is justified can depend on the absence of other atoms whose status is not
yet determined.
Interpretation provenance makes these dependencies explicit, recording not only
what conclusions are drawn, but the semantic commitments under which they are
valid.

We examine three standard semantics for negation—stratified, well-founded, and
stable—and write down the corresponding interpretation provenance in each case.
These distinctions will reappear in the next section, where ambiguity arises from
transaction schedules rather than negation.

\subsection{Specification and Semantic Ambiguity}

A Datalog program with negation induces a specification
$\Spec : \Hist \to 2^{O}$ mapping histories (EDB instances) to sets of admissible
outcomes (models).
Ambiguity arises because negation can create circular semantic dependencies:
whether an atom holds may depend on whether some other atom does not hold, and
vice versa.

\paragraph{Running example.}
Consider the following program $P$ over EDB facts in a history $H$:
\begin{align*}
  p &\leftarrow a.\\
  q &\leftarrow \neg p.\\[1mm]
  r &\leftarrow \neg s.\\
  s &\leftarrow \neg r.
\end{align*}
Assume $H$ contains the single extensional fact $a$.
The pair $(P,H)$ contains both a stratified fragment
($q$ depends negatively on $p$, and $p$ depends positively on $a$)
and a cycle through negation ($r \leftrightarrow \neg s$).
We use this single instance to exhibit, side-by-side, three distinct structures
of interpretation provenance.

\subsection{Interpretive Predicates for Negation}

Across all negation semantics we consider, interpretation proceeds by applying
interpretive predicates that constrain admissible models.
In this sandbox, it is helpful to think of interpretive predicates as semantic
commitments about the status of atoms---e.g., committing that an atom is true,
false, or (when the outcome domain is lifted) undefined.

These commitments are not local to individual derivations.
They depend on which outcomes remain admissible and may enable or disable further
commitments.
As a result, interpretive predicates for negation are ambiguity-sensitive and may
fail to commute.

A useful way to make this structure explicit is via a \emph{dependency graph} on
interpretive predicates.
An edge $\varphi \rightarrow \psi$ indicates that the commitment $\psi$ cannot be
made until $\varphi$ has been resolved.
Interpretation provenance records which such commitments must be fixed to obtain a
resolved specification on which ordinary (depth--0) derivational provenance is
defined.

\subsection{Stratified Semantics}

Stratified semantics applies to programs whose dependency graph contains no cycles
through negation.
It restricts attention to a class of specifications for which negation can be
interpreted by a stratum order.

For the stratified fragment of our example, $p$ lies in a lower stratum (derived
from EDB), and $q$ lies in a higher stratum (depending on $\neg p$).
The key semantic issue is not computing a fixpoint per se, but committing that
the lower stratum has \emph{stabilized}—i.e., that no additional lower-stratum
consequences remain that could change the truth of $p$.
In incremental or distributed settings, this stabilization is a genuine semantic
commitment: the possibility of late-arriving facts creates ambiguity about
whether negation is safe to apply.

Key interpretive predicates are $\varphi_{\mathrm{stab}(p)}$ (stabilization of the lower stratum defining $p$) and $\varphi_{\mathrm{neg}(q)}$ (the stratified negation commitment for $q$ given stabilization). Here the dependency is staged: $\varphi_{\mathrm{stab}(p)} \rightarrow \varphi_{\mathrm{neg}(q)}$. See Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-datalog} for $I_{\mathrm{strat}}$ and the corresponding conditioned provenance and interpretation provenance values.

\subsection{Well-Founded Semantics}

Well-founded semantics (WFS) applies to all Datalog programs with negation.
Rather than selecting among multiple total two-valued models, it resolves
ambiguity by admitting a richer outcome space in which atoms may be
\emph{true}, \emph{false}, or \emph{undefined}.
Undefinedness is not a failure of semantics; it is an explicit representation of
residual ambiguity due to negation cycles.

The staged prefix remains ($\varphi_{\mathrm{stab}(p)} \rightarrow \varphi_{\mathrm{neg}(q)}$). The cycle induces a global mutual dependence, but the resolution is deterministic (commit both to $\mathbf{u}$). Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-datalog} gives the explicit interpretation $I_{\mathrm{wfs}}$, the dependency displays, and the resulting conditioned provenance and interpretation provenance values.

\subsection{Stable Model Semantics}

Stable model semantics differs fundamentally from stratified and well-founded
semantics in how cycles of negation are resolved.

The staged prefix remains ($\varphi_{\mathrm{stab}(p)} \rightarrow \varphi_{\mathrm{neg}(q)}$). The same mutual dependence admits multiple resolving interpretations, requiring a semantic choice among them. Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-datalog} gives the explicit interpretations, dependency graphs, and provenance values.

\subsection{A Note on Acyclic Negation}

When a program is stratifiable, stratified, well-founded, and stable semantics
coincide in the sense that they yield the same resolved two-valued outcome.
In our running example, restricting attention to the stratified fragment
$\{p \leftarrow a.\;,\; q \leftarrow \neg p.\}$ eliminates the negation cycle and
recovers this coincidence.

From the perspective of interpretation provenance, this reflects the absence of
cycles of semantic dependency through negation: commitments can be staged by
strata, with stabilization commitments mediating the safe use of negation.

\subsection{Takeaways for Interpretation Provenance}

This sandbox highlights three distinct provenance shapes induced by three
classical negation semantics.
Stratification yields a layered structure in which negation-dependent derivations
are conditional on stabilization commitments for earlier strata.
Well-founded semantics resolves ambiguity by lifting the outcome domain and
recording explicit commitments to partiality (undefinedness) in the presence of
negation cycles.
Stable semantics yields multiple resolving interpretations for the same program
and instance, so interpretation provenance must record which resolved
interpretation was assumed even before derivational provenance is consulted.

Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-datalog} gives the explicit interpretations, dependency graphs, and provenance values.

\subsection{Semantic Change Example: Negation Semantics}

We illustrate semantic change with a concrete Datalog program whose conclusions
depend on how negation is resolved.

\paragraph{Program and instance.}
Consider the program $P$, consisting of a stratified fragment defining $p$, 
and a negation cycle between $q$ and $r$:
\[
\begin{array}{l}
p \;\drule\; a.\\
q \;\drule\; \neg r.\\
r \;\drule\; \neg q.
\end{array}
\]
with extensional instance $H = \{a\}$.


\paragraph{Well-founded semantics.}
Under well-founded semantics, the negation cycle is unresolved.
The unique resolving interpretation commits:
\[
I_{\mathrm{wfs}}
\;\triangleq\;
\varphi_{p=\mathbf{t}}
\;\wedge\;
\varphi_{q=\mathbf{u}}
\;\wedge\;
\varphi_{r=\mathbf{u}} .
\]
Over $\Spec \mid I_{\mathrm{wfs}}$, $p$ is true, while $q$ and $r$ are undefined.

Interpretation provenance records:
\[
	ext{Under } I_{\mathrm{wfs}},\ \mathsf{IP}(p) \triangleq (I_{\mathrm{wfs}},\, \Prov(a)),
\qquad
\mathsf{IP}(q) \text{ undefined},
\qquad
\mathsf{IP}(r) \text{ undefined}.
\]

\paragraph{Stable model semantics.}
Under stable model semantics, the same program admits two resolving interpretations:
\[
I^{(q)}_{\mathrm{stab}}
\;\triangleq\;
\varphi_{p=\mathbf{t}}
\wedge \varphi_{q=\mathbf{t}}
\wedge \varphi_{r=\mathbf{f}},
\qquad
I^{(r)}_{\mathrm{stab}}
\;\triangleq\;
\varphi_{p=\mathbf{t}}
\wedge \varphi_{r=\mathbf{t}}
\wedge \varphi_{q=\mathbf{f}} .
\]

In classical data provenance, the semantic outcome is fixed in advance, and
provenance refines explanations \emph{within} a single world.
Interpretation operates at a different level.
These resolving interpretations correspond to distinct admissible semantic
outcomes (or \emph{possible worlds}) of the same specification.
They are mutually incompatible: no single outcome can satisfy both
$I^{(q)}_{\mathrm{stab}}$ and $I^{(r)}_{\mathrm{stab}}$.
Interpretation therefore does not merely refine a world, it describes
\emph{which} world was chosen from a space of admissible worlds. In this example, interpretation provenance would record one of the following, depending on which stable model
was chosen:
\[
\mathsf{IP}(q) \triangleq (I^{(q)}_{\mathrm{stab}},\, 1),
\qquad
\mathsf{IP}(r) \triangleq (I^{(r)}_{\mathrm{stab}},\, 1).
\]



\paragraph{Robustness and contingency.}
Fact $p$ is \emph{robust}: it holds in every admissible semantic world under both
well-founded and stable semantics.
By contrast, $q$ and $r$ are \emph{semantically contingent}: each holds in some
admissible worlds but not others.
Their provenance depends on commitments that are:
(i) unavailable under well-founded semantics, and
(ii) mutually exclusive under stable semantics.

Interpretation provenance makes this dependence explicit.
The absence of $q$ under well-founded semantics is not a failure of derivation,
but the consequence of a different interpretation regime.
Likewise, the presence of $q$ under one stable model is incompatible with its
presence under the other.

\paragraph{Reassessment under semantic change.}
Interpretation provenance also supports reassessing conclusions drawn under one
semantic regime when another is adopted.
For example, a result derived under stable model semantics may be reconsidered
under well-founded semantics by checking whether its interpretation provenance
remains satisfied.
In this example, conclusions about $q$ or $r$ derived under a stable interpretation
have no counterpart under $I_{\mathrm{wfs}}$, while $p$ remains valid.
Section~\ref{sec:robustness} develops this notion of semantic change more systematically.

\vspace{1em}
In the next section, we will see closely analogous provenance shapes in
transactional systems: staging commitments (serializability orderings), lifted
outcome domains (snapshot-style visibility constraints), and multi-valued resolved
interpretations (multiple admissible serializations) over the same history.

\section{Sandbox II: Transactions as Systems Ambiguity}
\label{sec:transactions}
% ================================================================

We now turn to transactional systems.
As in the previous section, our goal is not to propose new correctness criteria,
but to understand how semantic ambiguity shapes provenance.

Transactional systems introduce a qualitatively different source of ambiguity
from negation.
Here, ambiguity arises from \emph{concurrency}: transactions race to read and write
shared state, and the execution does not uniquely determine which effects are
observed.
Interpretation provenance explains how query results depend not only on data, but
on the semantic resolution of that concurrency.

\subsection{Provenance Questions Under Concurrency}

Classical provenance explains how an output tuple depends on base data, assuming a
fixed semantics and a fixed execution outcome.
Transactional systems invalidate this assumption.

Consider a query result tuple $t$ produced by executing a workload of
transactions.
There are several distinct provenance questions one might ask: (\emph{Existence}) does $t$ appear under \emph{some} admissible execution; (\emph{Universality}) does $t$ appear under \emph{all} admissible executions; and (\emph{Dependence}) on which semantic choices---such as transaction orderings or visibility decisions---does the presence of $t$ depend?

These questions are strictly more subtle than asking whether a final database
state is invariant across executions.
A database state may vary across admissible schedules even when a particular
query answer is stable; conversely, a state may be fixed while a query result is
not.

Answering these questions requires provenance that is sensitive to
\emph{which transactional resolutions were relied upon}, not merely which data
items were read.
This is precisely the role of interpretation provenance.

\subsection{Ambiguity Before Order}

To address this setting, we begin with a deliberately
weak semantic model.
A transactional workload consists of a set of transactions, each issuing reads
and writes on shared objects and eventually committing.
At this level, the execution does not specify an order between transactions.
Reads may observe values written by different transactions, and multiple
explanations of the same observed behavior may coexist.

This lack of order is the source of semantic ambiguity.
Even with complete knowledge of all operations, multiple outcomes may be
admissible.
Order is not a primitive feature of the execution; it is a semantic commitment
used to justify an outcome.

\subsection{Outcomes and Serializability}

Under serializability, outcomes are defined via serialization.

An \emph{outcome} is a final database state produced by some serial execution of
the transactions.
An outcome is admissible if there exists a total order of transactions such that
executing them sequentially in that order produces the observed read values and yields the final state.

A single execution history may admit many such outcomes.
Each corresponds to a different semantic explanation of the same concurrent
behavior.

At this stage, no explanation is preferred.
Interpretation is required to select or constrain the admissible outcomes.

\subsection{Interpretive Predicates as Ordering Commitments}

Under serializability, semantic resolution proceeds by committing to relative
orderings between transactions.
Accordingly, the primitive interpretive predicates in this setting are
\emph{ordering predicates}.

For transactions $T_i$ and $T_j$, an ordering predicate $\prec(T_i, T_j)$
asserts that $T_i$ precedes $T_j$ in the serialization order.
Conditioning on $\prec(T_i, T_j)$ eliminates all admissible outcomes whose
serializations violate this ordering.

Interpretations are finite conjunctions of such ordering predicates.
Each interpretation therefore corresponds to a partial order on transactions,
refining the space of admissible serial executions.
A fully resolving interpretation induces a total order; partial interpretations
leave multiple serializations admissible.

Ordering predicates are ambiguity-sensitive.
Their effect depends on which serializations remain admissible, and different
ordering commitments may enable or preclude others.
As a result, ordering predicates need not commute, inducing semantic dependency
and interpretation depth.

\subsection{Semantic Change Example: Serializability}
\label{sec:txn-semantic-change}

We now revisit Example~\ref{ex:example} to illustrate how interpretation
provenance supports reasoning about \emph{semantic change} in transactional
systems.

\paragraph{Possible outcomes and incompatibility.}
Under serializability, the history in Example~\ref{ex:example} admits multiple
admissible outcomes.
In particular, tuple $d$ may or may not appear in the result of $Q$, depending on
how the insert and delete transactions are ordered relative to the query.

These outcomes are mutually incompatible.
No single serial execution can both include and exclude $d$ from the result.
Thus, as in the case of negation semantics, the specification admits multiple
distinct admissible semantic outcomes (possible worlds).

\paragraph{Resolving interpretations.}
As discussed in the example, we focus on interpretations in which the query
transaction $T_Q$ is the final transaction in the serialization, so that it
observes a complete database state.

Two resolving interpretations suffice:
\[
I_{\mathsf{in}}
\;\triangleq\;
\prec(T_4, T_Q) \;\wedge\; \prec(T_Q, T_5),
\qquad
I_{\mathsf{out}}
\;\triangleq\;
\prec(T_5, T_Q).
\]
Under $I_{\mathsf{in}}$, the insert of $d$ is visible to $T_Q$ and the delete is
not; under $I_{\mathsf{out}}$, the delete precedes the query and $d$ is absent.

These interpretations correspond to distinct admissible serializations of the
same concurrent history and are mutually incompatible. As in the case of negation semantics (Section~\ref{sec:negation}), interpretation
here does not refine explanations within a fixed outcome, but determines which
admissible outcome is being explained.

\paragraph{Interpretation provenance under serializability.}
Fixing an interpretation resolves the ambiguity and yields a depth--0 setting.

For tuples $b$, and $c$, the result is invariant:
\[
\Prov_{Q \mid I}(b) = x_1 + x_2,
\qquad
\Prov_{Q \mid I}(c) = x_3,
\]
for both $I_{\mathsf{in}}$ and $I_{\mathsf{out}}$.
Accordingly, $\mathsf{IP}_Q(b) \triangleq (I,\, \Prov_{Q \mid I}(b))$ and similarly for $c$.

Tuple $d$ is semantically contingent.
Under $I_{\mathsf{in}}$, the classical provenance question is \emph{why} $d$:
\[
\Prov_{Q \mid I_{\mathsf{in}}}(d) = x_4,
\qquad
\mathsf{IP}_Q(d) \triangleq (I_{\mathsf{in}},\, x_4).
\]
Under $I_{\mathsf{out}}$, $d$ is absent, and the appropriate classical question is
\emph{why-not} $d$, explained by the ordering commitment $\prec(T_5, T_Q)$.
There is no single classical provenance question for $d$ prior to fixing an
interpretation.

\paragraph{Semantic change.}
Interpretation provenance also supports reassessing conclusions under a change in
semantic assumptions.
For example, a result materialized under interpretation $I_{\mathsf{in}}$ can be
checked for validity under $I_{\mathsf{out}}$ by testing whether its interpretation
provenance remains satisfied.
In this example, conclusions about $b$ and $c$ persist, while conclusions
about $d$ do not.

This reassessment does not require recomputation of derivations.
It depends only on whether the semantic commitments recorded in the interpretation
provenance are compatible with the new interpretation.
Section~\ref{sec:robustness} generalizes this notion of semantic change and
robustness across families of interpretations.

\subsection{Interpretation Structure}

Once an interpretation is fixed---that is, once sufficient ordering commitments
have been made---query evaluation behaves monotonically.
Classical semiring provenance applies \emph{within} a fixed serialization.

Across serializations, however, results are conditional on ordering commitments.
A tuple's derivation may rely on particular ordering commitments, and the same
tuple may be justified under multiple admissible serializations.
This yields an interpretation whose structure reflects the staging of
transactional commitments.

\paragraph{Example: provenance of a query transaction.}
Consider three transactions:
$T_1$ inserts tuple $(1)$,
$T_2$ deletes tuple $(1)$,
and $T_Q$ executes query $Q$ that returns $(1)$ iff it is present.

Under serializability, the history admits multiple serializations.
Under a fixed serialization (equivalently, under a fixed resolving interpretation), the classical (depth-0) query provenance is:
\[
\Prov_Q =
\begin{cases}
\mathsf{id}(T_1) & \text{if } T_2 \prec T_1 \prec T_Q,\\
0                & \text{if } T_1 \prec T_2 \prec T_Q.
\end{cases}
\]

Interpretation provenance lifts this to the ambiguous history:
\[
I \;\triangleq\; \varphi_{T_2 \prec T_1} \wedge \varphi_{T_1 \prec T_Q},
\qquad
\Prov_{Q \mid I}(1)=\mathsf{id}(T_1),
\qquad
\mathsf{IP}_Q(1) \triangleq (I,\, \mathsf{id}(T_1)).
\]

In particular, interpretation provenance can distinguish tuples that appear in all admissible serializations, tuples that appear only under specific orderings, and tuples whose derivations depend on irreducible semantic choices.

This structure composes naturally with classical provenance:
query provenance explains \emph{why} a tuple follows from a given outcome,
while interpretation provenance explains \emph{under which outcomes} it holds.

\subsection{Beyond Serializability}

Other isolation levels expose the same phenomenon with different interpretive
predicates.

Snapshot isolation, for example, restricts admissible visibility relations while
preserving ambiguity.
Weaker isolation levels admit even larger families of outcomes.
In all cases, provenance questions remain the same:
which semantic commitments were required to justify a result?

Interpretation provenance provides a uniform account across these regimes.

\subsection{Summary}

Transactional systems introduce semantic ambiguity through concurrency.
Order is not primitive; it is a semantic commitment required to justify outcomes.

By foregrounding provenance questions---especially tuple-level questions about
dependence on semantic resolution---interpretation provenance extends classical
provenance to settings where correctness admits multiple valid executions.

\section{Change and Robustness Across Interpretations}
\label{sec:robustness}
% ================================================================

Interpretation provenance equips us with a semantic vocabulary that was previously
unavailable: it makes explicit not only derivations, but the semantic commitments
under which those derivations are valid.
With this vocabulary in hand, a new space of provenance questions becomes
well-posed.

Interpretation provenance also reframes why-not provenance.
Classical why-not provenance explains absence relative to a fixed semantics.
Under interpretation provenance, absence may instead be explained by the absence
of any resolving interpretation that justifies the result, or by the fact that
only interpretations ruled out by policy or specification choice would admit it.

In this section, we map this question space.
This section maps the provenance question space enabled by interpretation provenance; it does not propose algorithms or completeness results.
In particular, we focus on questions about change and robustness—how conclusions
behave as semantic assumptions vary, and which conclusions persist across
alternative interpretations.

\subsection{Semantic Change}

Many systems evolve over time.
Policies are revised, isolation levels are adjusted, and semantic regimes are
refined or relaxed.
Classical provenance is tied to a fixed semantics and therefore offers limited
support for reasoning across such changes.

In the interpretation framework, a change in semantics is naturally modeled as a
change in the resolving interpretation.
Let $\Spec$ be a fixed ambiguous specification, and let $I$ and $I'$ be two
resolving interpretations representing different semantic choices—for example,
different isolation levels, policy constraints, or negation semantics.

Interpretation provenance allows us to ask whether a conclusion $c$ is preserved
under semantic change:
\[
  c \in (\Spec \mid I)(H)
  \quad\text{and}\quad
  c \in (\Spec \mid I')(H).
\]
When this holds, $c$ is invariant under the change from $I$ to $I'$.

Because interpretation provenance records the semantic commitments under which
$c$ was derived, it can explain \emph{why} a conclusion fails to persist.
A conclusion may depend on commitments present in $I$ but absent from $I'$,
or vice versa.
This explanation is unavailable in classical provenance, which records only
derivational structure within a single resolved semantics.

\subsection{Robustness Across Interpretations}

Semantic change compares two specific interpretations.
A stronger notion is \emph{robustness}: whether a conclusion holds across an entire
family of admissible interpretations.

Let $\mathcal{I}$ be a class of resolving interpretations for $\Spec$—for example,
all serializable schedules, all stable models, or all interpretations compatible
with a given policy envelope.
We say that a conclusion $c$ is \emph{robust} with respect to $\mathcal{I}$ if
\[
  \forall I \in \mathcal{I},\quad c \in (\Spec \mid I)(H).
\]

Interpretation provenance provides a concrete handle on this notion.
Because provenance values are indexed by interpretations, robustness corresponds
to the absence of dependence on particular semantic commitments.
Intuitively, a robust conclusion is one whose justification does not rely on
choices that vary across $\mathcal{I}$.

This perspective separates two sources of variability:
\emph{derivational variability}, arising from alternative ways to derive a conclusion within a fixed interpretation, and \emph{semantic variability}, arising from alternative interpretations themselves.

Classical provenance addresses the former.
Interpretation provenance makes the latter explicit.

\subsubsection{Quantitative robustness.}
While we have treated robustness as a qualitative property—holding across all
interpretations in a class $\mathcal{I}$—interpretation provenance also supports
quantitative variants.

If $\mathcal{I}$ is finite or otherwise measurable, one may ask what fraction of
admissible interpretations justify a given conclusion.
This yields a probabilistic or statistical notion of robustness, analogous in
spirit to possible-worlds reasoning in probabilistic databases.

We emphasize that interpretation provenance does not require such a measure.
Rather, it provides the semantic structure needed to define and reason about
quantitative robustness when a distribution over interpretations is available or
assumed.

\subsection{Certificates and Robustness}

Layer certificates play a key role in reasoning about robustness.
Because certificates summarize exactly the semantic distinctions relevant to
subsequent interpretation, they provide a natural abstraction boundary.

If a conclusion’s provenance depends only on certificates that are invariant
across a class of interpretations, then the conclusion is robust with respect to
that class.
Conversely, dependence on interpretation-specific certificates pinpoints the
semantic commitments responsible for fragility.

This observation suggests a practical workflow:
identify the class of interpretations of interest, determine which certificates vary across that class, and examine which conclusions depend on those certificates.

We emphasize that this paper does not attempt to algorithmically enumerate
interpretations or compute robustness properties.
Rather, interpretation provenance provides the semantic structure needed to
formulate and justify such analyses.

\subsection{Discussion}

Reasoning about change and robustness reframes provenance as a tool not only for
explanation, but for semantic accountability.
By making resolution explicit, interpretation provenance allows systems to answer
questions about stability, sensitivity, and invariance that are increasingly
central in practice.

These questions arise across domains:
in transactional systems, where concurrency control choices affect outcomes;
in logic programming, where alternative semantics coexist;
and in policy-driven systems, where rules evolve over time.
Interpretation provenance offers a unified foundation for addressing them.

We view this section as a first step.
A full treatment of robustness—its computation, optimization, and integration
with existing provenance techniques—is an important direction for future work.

Interpretation provenance can also guard persisted results against semantic drift.
A result materialized under one interpretation (e.g., snapshot isolation) can be
checked for validity under another (e.g., serializability) by testing whether its
interpretation provenance remains satisfied.
This separates semantic soundness from recomputation.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% ================================================================

\subsection{Algebraic Provenance}

Algebraic provenance provides a unifying framework for explaining query results
under fixed, resolved semantics by annotating derivations with elements of a
commutative semiring.
The foundational work of Green et al.~\cite{green2007provenance} showed how
why-provenance, how-provenance, and lineage could be subsumed under a single
algebraic abstraction.
This perspective has since been extended to probabilistic databases, incomplete
information, aggregation, and factorized representations
\cite{suciu2011probabilistic,dalvi2012probabilistic,olteanu2015factorized,senellart2018provenance}.

Our work builds directly on this foundation, but shifts the focus from derivations
under a fixed semantics to the semantic conditions under which such derivations
are meaningful.
Rather than extending the algebra itself, we study when algebraic provenance
becomes applicable in the presence of semantic ambiguity.
Interpretation provenance makes explicit the semantic commitments required to
reach a resolved setting and explains how classical semiring provenance depends on
those commitments.
Viewed this way, classical algebraic provenance corresponds to the depth--0
fragment of a broader semantic framework.

\subsection{Negation and Non-Monotonic Semantics}

Non-monotonic logic programming, and Datalog with negation in particular, provides
the canonical mathematically explicit setting for studying semantic ambiguity and
its resolution.
Stratified semantics~\cite{apt1988semantics,przymusinski1988semantics},
well-founded semantics~\cite{vanGelder1991wellFounded}, and stable model semantics
\cite{gelfond1988stable} offer distinct, well-understood approaches to resolving
negation, with known relationships under syntactic restrictions such as
stratifiability.

Our contribution is not to propose new semantics for negation, but to reinterpret
these classical approaches through the lens of interpretation provenance.
Datalog with negation serves as a control experiment: ambiguity is semantic rather
than operational, resolution strategies are formal objects, and equivalence
results are well established.
This makes it an ideal setting in which to study how semantic resolution shapes
provenance structure.
A deeper treatment of Datalog$^{\neg}$ as a design space for semantic resolution
appears in companion work.

\subsection{Provenance, Explanations, and Counterfactuals}

A large body of work studies explanations and causality for query answers,
including why-not provenance, causal responsibility, and counterfactual
explanations
\cite{buneman2001why,halpern2001causes,meliou2010causality,glavic2015survey}.
These approaches focus on explaining results relative to a fixed semantics, often
by identifying minimal subsets of input facts or derivations.

Interpretation provenance is complementary.
Rather than explaining why a result follows from a chosen semantics, it explains
which semantic assumptions were required for the result to hold at all.
In this sense, interpretation provenance lifts explanation from the derivational
level to the semantic level, making explicit which conclusions are robust across
interpretations and which depend on particular resolution choices.

\subsection{Transactional and Systems Provenance}

A first line of work studies provenance in transactional and workflow settings
under a fixed execution or schedule.
This includes classical workflow provenance, audit trails, and dependency graphs,
as well as database-oriented treatments of transactional behavior under assumed
isolation guarantees
\cite{bernstein1987concurrency,adya1999weak,cerone2015consistency,cheney2009provenance,freire2008provenance}.
In these settings, the semantic resolution is implicit: provenance explains results
\emph{relative to a chosen execution or snapshot}.
From the perspective of this paper, such approaches operate at interpretation
depth~0: ambiguity has already been resolved, and provenance records derivations
within that resolution.

A distinct body of systems work studies \emph{execution provenance} or
\emph{systems lineage}, aiming to explain observed behavior by recording causal
dependencies among processes, files, messages, and events
\cite{seltzer2005provenance,bates2015trustworthy}.
Distributed tracing systems such as Dapper reconstruct request paths and critical
dependencies from partial observations to support debugging and performance
diagnosis \cite{sigelman2010dapper}.
Here, provenance confronts a deeper form of ambiguity: the same observed history
may permit multiple admissible executions or reconstructions, depending on ordering,
timing, or alignment assumptions.

Interpretation provenance informs this second line of work.
Rather than recording a single reconstructed execution, it makes explicit the
\emph{semantic commitments} required for a diagnostic conclusion to hold across
admissible executions.
This elevates systems lineage from execution-relative explanation to
interpretation-aware explanation, and introduces formal notions of parsimony and
robustness.
As illustrated in Appendix~\ref{app:systems-lineage}, interpretation provenance
provides a minimal, explicit account of which assumptions are required to justify
systems-level conclusions, complementing existing lineage mechanisms rather than
replacing them.

\subsection{Abstraction, Quotients, and Semantic Resolution}

Quotienting and abstraction play a central role in semantic theory, from abstract
interpretation~\cite{cousot1977abstract} to program equivalence and refinement.
In database theory, quotient constructions arise implicitly in determinacy,
view-based reasoning, and consistency models, often without being surfaced as
objects of explanation.

In our framework, interpretations induce quotients on outcome spaces by
restricting and identifying admissible outcomes until ambiguity is resolved.
Interpretation provenance explains how these quotients arise and which semantic
distinctions they preserve.
This perspective complements existing abstraction-based approaches by making
semantic resolution explicit and explainable, rather than implicit in the model.

\subsection{Interpretation Complexity}

This work builds on a prior complexity theory of interpretation introduced
in~\cite{hellerstein2026interpretation}, which characterizes the cost and depth of
semantic resolution and identifies when interpretation requires irreducible
staging or coordination.

The present paper focuses instead on explanation.
We assume interpretations are given and ask how provenance can explain results
under those interpretations.
Together, the two lines of work provide a structural and explanatory account of
semantic resolution: complexity characterizes what resolution requires, while
interpretation provenance explains how it was used.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ================================================================

This paper reframes provenance around a simple observation: many modern systems
do not produce a single semantic outcome from a history, but a space of admissible
ones.
In such settings, explaining a result requires accounting for how ambiguity was
resolved—not merely how derivations proceeded after the fact.
Interpretation provenance makes those semantic commitments explicit and composable,
recovering classical algebraic provenance once resolution is fixed, while
retaining visibility into the assumptions that made resolution possible.

Our development shows that this perspective is not tied to a particular domain.
In transactional systems, interpretation provenance explains how query results
depend on ordering and isolation commitments.
In logic programming with negation, it clarifies the structure of semantic
dependencies that have long been studied but rarely treated as provenance objects.
Across domains, certificates provide a minimal interface for carrying forward just
the information required to support further reasoning.

More importantly, interpretation provenance opens a broader research agenda.

\paragraph{Robustness and semantic sensitivity.}
Once results are annotated with the interpretations under which they hold, it
becomes natural to ask which conclusions are robust across interpretations and
which are fragile.
This suggests new provenance questions—about semantic stability, policy change,
and compatibility across regimes—that go beyond traditional why- and how-provenance.
Developing principled robustness measures, including probabilistic or lattice-based
summaries over interpretations, is a promising direction.

\paragraph{Engineering interpretation provenance.}
While interpretations are minimal by construction, certificates admit multiple
representations.
Designing compact, efficient certificates—tailored to particular domains,
workloads, or performance goals—raises new engineering questions at the boundary
of semantics and systems.
This includes incremental maintenance, compression, and interaction with existing
provenance stores.

\paragraph{Beyond databases.}
Semantic ambiguity and resolution are not unique to data management.
Shared-memory consistency models, replica coordination protocols, access-control
policies, and even machine learning pipelines exhibit similar patterns of
underspecification followed by structured commitment.
Interpretation provenance provides a vocabulary for transporting insights across
these domains, suggesting a unifying foundation for provenance in ambiguous
computational settings.

The original success of algebraic provenance lay in identifying the right semantic
object to explain.
Interpretation provenance extends that insight: when meaning itself depends on
resolution, provenance must explain the assumptions that made meaning possible.
We hope this work provides both a foundation and an invitation to explore that
space.

\section{Appendix: Worked Examples}
\label{sec:appendix-examples}

\subsection{Full one-shot transactional provenance example}
\label{sec:appendix-concurrency}

We begin with a minimal example that already requires interpretation provenance.

\paragraph{Data, query, and history.}
Let $S(k,y)$ be a relation and consider the set-valued query $Q(y) : \exists k.\; S(k,y)$.
The database initially contains $S(0,a)$ with provenance variable $x_a$.
Five transactions execute:
\[
\begin{array}{ll}
T_1: \textsf{insert } S(1,b) \ (x_1) \qquad &
T_2: \textsf{insert } S(2,b) \ (x_2)\\
T_3: \textsf{insert } S(3,c) \ (x_3) \qquad &
T_4: \textsf{insert } S(4,d) \ (x_4)\\
T_5: \textsf{delete } S(4,d). &
\end{array}
\]
A query transaction $T_Q$ evaluates $Q$ under a
serializable execution model, where the visibility of updates is
determined by the chosen serialization order.

\paragraph{Semantic ambiguity.}
The history alone does not determine whether $d$ is visible to $T_Q$.
That depends on how $T_Q$ is ordered relative to $T_4$ and $T_5$.
Write $\varphi_{T_i \prec T_j}$ for the interpretive predicate committing
that the schedule that ensued corresponds to equivalent serial schedule in which
$T_i$ committed prior to $T_j$.

Transactions $T_1,T_2,T_3$ conflict with the query $Q$ and therefore must
be ordered with respect to $T_Q$ in any serializable execution. 
For expository purposes, we restrict attention to serializable executions
in which $T_Q$ is last in the serialization; this loses no generality for
the example, and allows the query result to serve as a witness of the final
database state.
Transactions $T_4$ and $T_5$ conflict with each other and with $T_Q$,
leaving their relative ordering unresolved.

Given that analysis, two resolving interpretations suffice:
\[
\begin{aligned}
  I_{\mathsf{in}} &\;\triangleq\; \varphi_{T_4 \prec T_Q} \wedge \varphi_{T_Q \prec T_5} &&\quad\text{(observe insert before delete)}\\
  I_{\mathsf{out}} &\;\triangleq\; \varphi_{T_5 \prec T_Q} &&\quad\text{(observe after delete).}
\end{aligned}
\]

\paragraph{Depth--0 provenance under each interpretation.}
Conditioned on $I_{\mathsf{in}}$, the resolved snapshot contains $b,c,d$:
\[
  \Prov_{Q \mid I_{\mathsf{in}}}(b)=x_1+x_2,\;
  \Prov_{Q \mid I_{\mathsf{in}}}(c)=x_3,\;
  \Prov_{Q \mid I_{\mathsf{in}}}(d)=x_4.
\]
Conditioned on $I_{\mathsf{out}}$, the snapshot contains $b,c$ but not $d$.

\paragraph{Why vs.\ why-not depends on interpretation.}
Under $I_{\mathsf{in}}$, the natural classical question for $d$ is \emph{why},
answered by $x_4$.
Under $I_{\mathsf{out}}$, the natural question is \emph{why-not} $d$: the witness
$k=4$ fails because $S(4,d)$ is false in the resolved snapshot.
There is no single classical provenance question for $d$ prior to fixing an
interpretation.

\paragraph{Interpretation provenance.}
Interpretation provenance attaches the resolving interpretation to the ordinary
derivational explanation.
For the observed output $\{b,c\}$, we record
\[
  \mathsf{IP}_Q(b)\triangleq (I,\, x_1+x_2),\quad
  \mathsf{IP}_Q(c)\triangleq (I,\, x_3),
\]
where $I$ is the interpretation assumed by the system (e.g., $I_{\mathsf{out}}$).

This single representation supports both why- and why-not reasoning.
Under $I_{\mathsf{in}}$, $d$ admits a why-provenance explanation; under
$I_{\mathsf{out}}$, it admits a why-not explanation.
Interpretation provenance makes this dependence explicit.

\subsection{Datalog\texorpdfstring{$^{\neg}$}{ with negation} worked interpretation provenance}
\label{sec:appendix-datalog}

\subsubsection{Stratified semantics: explicit interpretation provenance}

\paragraph{Interpretation provenance (example).}
Write $\varphi_{\mathrm{stab}(p)}$ for the interpretive predicate that commits
that the lower stratum defining $p$ has stabilized for the current history, and
$\varphi_{\mathrm{neg}(q)}$ for the predicate that commits to the stratified
reading of $q \leftarrow \neg p$ \emph{given} that commitment.
A resolving interpretation for the stratified fragment can be written
\[
  I_{\mathrm{strat}} \;\triangleq\;
  \varphi_{\mathrm{stab}(p)} \wedge \varphi_{\mathrm{neg}(q)}.
\]

The interpretive predicate dependency is strict:
\[
  \varphi_{\mathrm{stab}(p)} \;\rightarrow\; \varphi_{\mathrm{neg}(q)}.
\]
The negation commitment cannot be applied until stabilization of the lower stratum
has been resolved.

Over the resolved specification $\Spec \mid I_{\mathrm{strat}}$, $p$ is derived
from $a$ and has ordinary derivation provenance
$k_p \triangleq \Prov(a)$.
Interpretation provenance records that this derivation is justified under the
stabilization-and-negation commitments:
\[
  \mathsf{IP}(p) \;\triangleq\; (I_{\mathrm{strat}},\, k_p) .
\]

In contrast, $q$ is determined only after $\varphi_{\mathrm{stab}(p)}$ is in
force; under our $H$ with $a$, $q$ does not hold under stratified semantics.

\subsubsection{Well-founded semantics: explicit interpretation provenance}

In our example, the stratified fragment induces the same prefix dependencies as
before:
\[
  \varphi_{\mathrm{stab}(p)} \;\rightarrow\; \varphi_{\mathrm{neg}(q)}.
\]
Beyond this prefix, the remaining atoms participate in a negation cycle.
Neither $r$ nor $s$ is justified without circular reasoning, so WFS commits both
to \emph{undefined}.

\paragraph{Interpretation provenance (example).}
Let $\mathbf{t},\mathbf{f},\mathbf{u}$ denote the well-founded truth values
true/false/undefined.
A resolving interpretation for $(P,H)$ under WFS can be written schematically as
\[
  I_{\mathrm{wfs}} \;\triangleq\;
  \varphi_{p=\mathbf{t}} \wedge \varphi_{q=\mathbf{f}}
  \wedge \varphi_{r=\mathbf{u}} \wedge \varphi_{s=\mathbf{u}} .
\]

The interpretive predicate dependency graph therefore consists of a stratified
prefix followed by a cycle:
\[
  \varphi_{\mathrm{stab}(p)} \;\rightarrow\; \varphi_{\mathrm{neg}(q)}
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  \varphi_{r=\mathbf{u}} \;\leftrightarrow\; \varphi_{s=\mathbf{u}} .
\]
The cycle admits no precedence ordering.
Well-founded semantics resolves it deterministically by committing \emph{both}
atoms to $\mathbf{u}$.

Over $\Spec \mid I_{\mathrm{wfs}}$, derivational provenance is depth--0; in
particular $p$ again has
$k_p \triangleq \Prov(a)$.
Interpretation provenance records the semantic commitments required for that
derivation:
\[
  \mathsf{IP}(p) \;\triangleq\; (I_{\mathrm{wfs}},\, k_p) .
\]

The distinctive feature is that $I_{\mathrm{wfs}}$ resolves the negation cycle
without making a two-valued choice: it commits explicitly to leaving $r$ and $s$
undefined.

\subsubsection{Stable semantics: explicit interpretation provenance}

As before, the stratified fragment induces the same prefix dependencies:
\[
  \varphi_{\mathrm{stab}(p)} \;\rightarrow\; \varphi_{\mathrm{neg}(q)}.
\]
Beyond this prefix, the negation cycle admits multiple two-valued resolutions.

For our example with $a$, the stratified fragment forces $p$ true and $q$ false,
while the cycle admits two stable outcomes: one with $r$ true and $s$ false, and
one with $s$ true and $r$ false.

\paragraph{Interpretation provenance (example).}
Accordingly, stable semantics yields multiple resolving interpretations for the
same $(P,H)$.
Write
\[
  I_{\mathrm{stab}}^{(r)} \triangleq
  \varphi_{p=\mathbf{t}} \wedge \varphi_{q=\mathbf{f}}
  \wedge \varphi_{r=\mathbf{t}} \wedge \varphi_{s=\mathbf{f}}
\]
and
\[
  I_{\mathrm{stab}}^{(s)} \triangleq
  \varphi_{p=\mathbf{t}} \wedge \varphi_{q=\mathbf{f}}
  \wedge \varphi_{s=\mathbf{t}} \wedge \varphi_{r=\mathbf{f}} .
\]

The dependency structure is therefore:
\[
  \varphi_{\mathrm{stab}(p)} \;\rightarrow\; \varphi_{\mathrm{neg}(q)},
  \qquad
  \varphi_{r=\mathbf{t}} \;\leftrightarrow\; \varphi_{s=\mathbf{f}},
  \qquad
  \varphi_{s=\mathbf{t}} \;\leftrightarrow\; \varphi_{r=\mathbf{f}} .
\]
Unlike WFS, stable semantics does not prescribe a unique resolution of the cycle;
each choice yields a distinct resolved interpretation.

Within either resolved outcome, derivational provenance is ordinary:
$p$ again has $k_p \triangleq \Prov(a)$, so
\[
  \mathsf{IP}(p) \;\triangleq\; (I_{\mathrm{stab}}^{(r)},\, k_p)
  \quad\text{or}\quad
  \mathsf{IP}(p) \;\triangleq\; (I_{\mathrm{stab}}^{(s)},\, k_p) ,
\]
depending on which stable interpretation is assumed.
The cycle atoms exhibit genuinely interpretation-dependent conclusions; e.g.,
\[
  \mathsf{IP}(r) \;\triangleq\; (I_{\mathrm{stab}}^{(r)},\, 1)
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  \mathsf{IP}(s) \;\triangleq\; (I_{\mathrm{stab}}^{(s)},\, 1) .
\]
Thus, in the stable setting, interpretation provenance must account explicitly for
which \emph{resolved interpretation} was selected.

\section{Appendix: Interpretation Provenance for Systems Lineage}
\label{app:systems-lineage}
% ================================================================

This appendix illustrates interpretation provenance in a systems setting that
lies outside traditional database semantics.
The example is adapted directly from the Dapper distributed tracing model
introduced by Sigelman et al.~\cite{sigelman2010dapper}, abstracting its trace
semantics rather than its implementation details.

The example serves two purposes.
First, it shows that interpretation provenance extends the database tradition of
provenance to forms of \emph{systems lineage} that have historically resisted
formal treatment.
Second, it demonstrates how the parsimony of interpretations---as minimal semantic
commitments---can refine systems lineage explanations by making explicit which
assumptions are required to justify diagnostic conclusions.

\subsection{Trace Specification and Semantic Ambiguity}

A distributed trace consists of a set of \emph{spans}, each representing an
invocation of a service.
Each span $X$ has a client-side send and receive event
$(\mathsf{cs}_X,\mathsf{cr}_X)$ and a server-side receive and send event
$(\mathsf{sr}_X,\mathsf{ss}_X)$.
Client and server events occur on different hosts with unsynchronized clocks.

The trace specification $\Spec$ maps a history $H$ of timestamped span events to a
set of admissible executions $O$.
An execution assigns each span a concrete placement on a global timeline and
induces a partial order over spans.
The specification enforces only causal constraints:
\[
  \mathsf{cs}_X \prec \mathsf{sr}_X
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  \mathsf{ss}_X \prec \mathsf{cr}_X ,
\]
together with parent--child relationships recorded in the trace metadata.

Because clocks are unsynchronized, these constraints determine only \emph{bounds}
on server-side timestamps.
As a result, multiple executions---with different relative orderings and overlaps
of spans---are admissible under the same observed history.
Critical-path analysis is therefore semantically ambiguous prior to interpretation.

\subsection{Diagnostic Query: Critical Path}

We consider the diagnostic query:
\begin{quote}
\emph{Which span lies on the critical path of the request?}
\end{quote}
Formally, the query maps an execution to the span (or set of spans) whose duration
determines the end-to-end latency of the root span.

Under a fixed execution, this query is deterministic.
Under the specification $\Spec$, however, different admissible executions may
yield different critical paths.

\subsection{Interpretive Predicates}

Interpretation resolves this ambiguity by committing to semantic assumptions that
fix a particular execution.
Relevant interpretive predicates include:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\varphi_{\mathrm{align}(h_i,h_j)}$, committing to a concrete clock
        alignment between hosts $h_i$ and $h_j$ within the admissible bounds;
  \item $\varphi_{\mathrm{order}(X,Y)}$, committing that span $X$ precedes span
        $Y$ when their relative order is otherwise unconstrained;
  \item $\varphi_{\mathrm{cp}=X}$, committing that $X$ is the critical-path
        witness once ordering and alignment are fixed.
\end{itemize}

These predicates are ambiguity-sensitive and may be semantically dependent.
For example, committing to $\varphi_{\mathrm{cp}=X}$ presupposes commitments that
fix the relative placement of competing spans.

\subsection{Resolving Interpretations}

Let $B$ and $C$ be sibling spans whose relative order is not determined by causal
constraints.
Two resolving interpretations suffice to illustrate the provenance structure:
\[
  I_B \;\triangleq\;
  \varphi_{\mathrm{align}} \wedge \varphi_{\mathrm{order}(B,C)} \wedge
  \varphi_{\mathrm{cp}=B},
\]
\[
  I_C \;\triangleq\;
  \varphi_{\mathrm{align}} \wedge \varphi_{\mathrm{order}(C,B)} \wedge
  \varphi_{\mathrm{cp}=C}.
\]
Both interpretations resolve $\Spec$, but they induce different critical-path
outcomes.

\subsection{Interpretation Provenance}

Once an interpretation is fixed, critical-path computation is ordinary.
Let $k_B$ (resp.\ $k_C$) denote the derivational justification that $B$ (resp.\ $C$)
is the longest-duration span in the resolved execution.
Interpretation provenance records:
\[
  \mathsf{IP}(\mathrm{cp}=B) \;\triangleq\; (I_B,\, k_B),
  \qquad
  \mathsf{IP}(\mathrm{cp}=C) \;\triangleq\; (I_C,\, k_C) .
\]

The provenance makes explicit that the diagnostic conclusion depends on semantic
commitments about clock alignment and span ordering.
Without fixing an interpretation, the question ``which span is on the critical
path?'' is ill-posed.

\subsection{Certificates and Parsimony}

Notably, the full trace history is unnecessary to justify the diagnosis.
A sufficient certificate consists of:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the parent--child span structure;
  \item the inequality constraints induced by send/receive events;
  \item the specific alignment and ordering commitments recorded in $I_B$ or
        $I_C$.
\end{itemize}

Interpretation provenance therefore supports a compressed form of systems lineage:
only the semantic commitments required for the diagnostic conclusion need be
retained.
This refines existing tracing practice by separating observable evidence from the
interpretive assumptions used to explain it.

\paragraph{Summary.}
This example demonstrates that interpretation provenance applies naturally to
systems diagnostics.
It extends provenance beyond data derivations to explanations of execution-level
properties, while retaining a minimal, explicit account of the semantic
assumptions required to justify those explanations.
