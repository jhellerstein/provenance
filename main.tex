\begin{abstract}
  Algebraic provenance traces derivations under a fixed, resolved semantics.
  Despite its theoretical elegance, this foundation has had limited impact on
  the systems that most need explanation---transactional databases, distributed
  services, concurrent pipelines---because these systems operate under
  \emph{semantic ambiguity}: multiple consistent executions, orderings, or
  isolation levels may be admissible for the same history.

  We introduce two new algebraic objects that extend provenance to this
  setting.
  \emph{Conditioned provenance} explains a query result under a single
  determination; its algebraic structure is a \emph{commitment tower}
  (a sequence of commutative monoids resting on a provenance semiring).
  \emph{Determination provenance} collects conditioned provenance across all
  determinations into a $K$-relation, whose supports form a
  commutative semiring---recovering the semiring framework of Green et al.\
  at a new level.
  Classical provenance is the degenerate case where no commitments are needed.
  We develop the framework in depth for transactional systems, where ambiguity
  arises from concurrency and isolation choices: determination provenance
  distinguishes outcomes that hold under every serializable execution from
  those contingent on a particular scheduling or isolation decision, with
  depth governed by conflict-graph topology.
  We further instantiate the framework for Datalog with negation---a
  longstanding gap in provenance past the monotone fragment---and for
  distributed systems observability, demonstrating the generality of the
  approach.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Algebraic provenance has given database theory a powerful and durable language for
explaining query results.
When a query has well-defined semantics---a single correct answer for each
input---provenance traces derivations through a commutative semiring, supporting
why- and how-provenance, uncertainty propagation, and
optimization~\cite{green2007provenance,suciu2011probabilistic}.
Explanation reduces to arithmetic, and this reduction has been remarkably
effective.

Despite this success, formal data provenance has had limited impact on the
systems that most need explanation: transactional databases, distributed
services, and concurrent pipelines.
These systems record execution traces and lineage
metadata~\cite{sigelman2010dapper,seltzer2005provenance,bates2015trustworthy},
but lack the algebraic foundation that makes database provenance effective.
The obstacle is a semantic mismatch: these systems operate under
\emph{semantic ambiguity}---a transactional workload may admit multiple
serializable executions; a distributed system may admit multiple orderings
consistent with the same observed history---and classical provenance theory
presupposes the very resolution that these systems leave open.

This mismatch gives rise to provenance questions that have no analog in the
classical setting.
Did a query result appear as an inevitable consequence of the data, or because
of a particular scheduling choice?
Would it survive a change from snapshot isolation to serializability?
The same phenomenon arises in database theory: a Datalog program with
negation may admit multiple models, and whether a derived tuple holds
may depend on which model is chosen.

This paper extends provenance to settings with semantic ambiguity.
The key idea is simple: before we can explain \emph{how} a result was
derived, we must first account for \emph{which semantic commitments} made
that derivation possible.
A transactional system that committed $T_2$ before $T_3$ made a choice;
a different commit order would have produced a different result with
different provenance.
We call such a sequence of irrevocable commitments a \emph{determination}.

Once a determination is fixed, classical provenance applies---the ambiguity
is gone and derivations can be traced through a semiring as usual.
The interesting structure lives in two places.
First, \emph{within} a single determination: commitments may depend on each
other (later commitments may be valid only after earlier ones are
discharged), giving rise to a layered structure we call the
\emph{commitment tower}.
Second, \emph{across} determinations: different determinations may yield
different outcomes, and tracking which determinations support a given outcome
yields a semiring structure---the \emph{determination semiring}---that
composes through queries in the same way that Green et al.'s provenance
semiring composes through relational algebra~\cite{green2007provenance}.

  We instantiate the framework in depth for transactional systems, where
  ambiguity arises from concurrency and isolation choices.
  Determination provenance distinguishes outcomes that hold under every
  admissible execution from those contingent on a particular commit ordering
  or isolation decision.
  We further demonstrate generality through two appendix instantiations:
  Datalog with negation (addressing a longstanding gap in provenance past
  the monotone fragment~\cite{green2007provenance}) and distributed systems
  observability~\cite{sigelman2010dapper}.
  We begin with a transactional example:

\begin{example}[Provenance under transactional ambiguity]
  \label{ex:example}
  Consider a relation $S(k,y)$ with initial contents $\{S(0,b)\}$.
  Three transactions execute concurrently with a query:
  \[
    T_1\!: \textsf{ins } S(1,b);\qquad
    T_2\!: \textsf{ins } S(2,d);\qquad
    T_3\!: \textsf{del } S(2,d);\qquad
    T_Q\!: Q(y) \drule S(k,y).
  \]
  All four are concurrent; any interleaving of their operations is possible.

  Value $b$ appears in $Q$'s result regardless of how the operations are
  ordered: the initial tuple $S(0,b)$ is never deleted.
  Thus $b$ is \emph{robust}.
  Value $d$ is \emph{contingent}: it appears if $T_2$'s insert takes effect
  before $T_Q$'s read and $T_3$'s delete takes effect after, but is absent
  if $T_3$'s delete precedes $T_Q$'s read.
  Before resolving this ambiguity, even the \emph{form} of the provenance
  question for $d$ is undetermined: in one determination the relevant
  question is \emph{why} $d$ appears, while in another
  it is \emph{why-not} $d$.
  Determination provenance makes this dependence explicit by recording the
  commitments under which the query result is determined, together with the
  derivational explanation that applies once those commitments are fixed.
  Section~\ref{sec:transactions} formalizes this example.
\end{example}

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item \emph{Conditioned provenance} and the \emph{commitment tower}:
        a structural decomposition of provenance under semantic ambiguity
        into layers of commitment (commutative monoids) discharging
        sequentially until a classical provenance semiring is exposed
        (Section~\ref{sec:conditional-semiring}).
  \item \emph{Determination provenance} and the \emph{determination
        semiring}: the algebraic structure across determinations, recovering
        Green et al.'s semiring framework at a new level
        (Section~\ref{sec:det-semiring}).
  \item Instantiation in transactional systems, with determination depth
        governed by conflict-graph topology
        (Section~\ref{sec:transactions}); and in Datalog
        with negation, addressing robustness across semantics and why-not
        provenance under ambiguity
        (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-datalog}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Preliminaries: Ambiguous Semantics and Refinement}
\label{sec:prelim}
% ================================================================

Classical data provenance assumes a fixed, resolved semantics: for each input
instance, a query determines a unique result set, and provenance explains how
each tuple in that set was derived.
In many systems, however, concurrency, negation, or underspecification admit
multiple admissible results for the same input.

This section introduces a minimal semantic framework for reasoning about
ambiguity.
We model specifications with multiple admissible outcomes and the
commitments—called \emph{determinations}—that restrict ambiguity until
meaning is determinate.

\subsection{Histories}

\begin{definition}[History]
  A \emph{history} is a finite partially ordered set
  $
    H = (E, \rightarrow),
  $
  where $E$ is a set of events and $\rightarrow \subseteq E \times E$ is a strict
  (irreflexive and transitive) partial order representing known precedence constraints among events.
\end{definition}

\noindent
A history represents a snapshot of what has happened so far in one
particular run of a system: which events have occurred and which
preceded which.
The class $\Hist$ of all histories represents all possible states across
all possible runs.
When the partial order is total, a history is a sequence (e.g., a
transaction schedule).
A database instance for classical provenance would naturally be represented
as a history of unordered tuple insertions: that history ``initializes''
the instance.

\begin{definition}[History Extension]
  For histories $H_1=(E_1,\rightarrow_1)$ and $H_2=(E_2,\rightarrow_2)$, we write
  $H_1 \sqsubseteq H_2$ if:
  \begin{enumerate}[nosep,label=(\arabic*)]
    \item $E_1 \subseteq E_2$, and
    \item $\rightarrow_1 = \rightarrow_2 \cap (E_1 \times E_1)$, and
    \item no event in $E_1$ has a predecessor in $E_2 \setminus E_1$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

Intuitively, $\sqsubseteq$ represents information growth: an extension may
add new events (and precedence edges involving them) but may not insert new
predecessors of existing events.
For a transaction schedule, new operations cannot be placed before existing
ones; for an unordered history of tuple insertions, new insertions (or
deletions) may be added but cannot be ordered before existing ones.

\subsection{Outcomes and Specifications}
An \emph{outcome} is an element of a set $O$ of externally
observable results (e.g., a query result, a database state, or a model of a
logic program).

\begin{definition}[Specification]
  A \emph{specification} is a relation $\Spec \subseteq \Hist \times O$:
  it associates each history with a set of \emph{admissible outcomes}.
  We write $\Spec(H) \triangleq \{o \in O \mid (H,o) \in \Spec\}$ for the
  admissible set at~$H$.
\end{definition}

\noindent
A specification is a \emph{relation}: a given input (history) may map to
multiple admissible outcomes, reflecting unresolved semantic ambiguity.
A specification that maps every history to exactly one outcome is a
\emph{function}---no ambiguity remains.
The goal of determination is to narrow a relational specification to a
functional one.

\begin{definition}[Determined specification]
  \label{def:determined}
  A specification is \emph{determined} if it is a function:
  $|\Spec(H)| = 1$ for every $H \in \Hist$.
\end{definition}


\subsection{Semantic Refinement}

Ambiguity is constrained by making additional semantic commitments.
We model commitments as constraints on the admissible set that
take effect at specific points in the history.

\begin{definition}[Commitment]
  \label{def:commitment}%
  A \emph{commitment} $\varphi$ is a function on outcome sets:
  given a history $H$ with admissible set $\Spec(H)$, applying $\varphi$
  produces a nonempty subset
  $\Spec(H \cdot \varphi) \subseteq \Spec(H)$.
  We write $H \cdot \varphi$ for the history obtained by
  adding a commitment event $\varphi$ after all maximal
  events of~$H$
  (i.e., $e \to \varphi$ for every $e$ with no successor
  in~$H$), giving $H \sqsubseteq H \cdot \varphi$.
  The result may depend on the full set $\Spec(H)$, not only on
  items in $\Spec(H)$ individually---as a result, commitments may be
  non-commutative.
  We require \emph{shrinkage}: $\Spec(H') \subseteq \Spec(H)$ for every
  $H' \sqsupseteq H \cdot \varphi$.
\end{definition}

\noindent
A commitment event succeeds all maximal events of~$H$, inducing a cut in the
history: everything before the commitment is fixed, and the admissible set
after the commitment can only be smaller.
We write \mbox{$(\Spec \mid \varphi)(H) \triangleq \Spec(H \cdot \varphi)$}:
the admissible outcomes after appending $\varphi$ to $H$.

\paragraph{Commitment bases.}
A \emph{commitment basis} $\Phi$ is the vocabulary of constraints available
for narrowing a specification---the set of conditions from which
determinations are formed.
The choice of basis determines which distinctions can be expressed and at
what granularity.
In Datalog with stratified negation, the natural constraints are sealing
conditions (asserting that a stratum is complete); in transactional systems
they are commit and abort decisions (Section~\ref{sec:transactions}),
optionally enriched with scheduling constraints
(Section~\ref{sec:scheduling-basis}).
Different bases for the same specification yield different determination
structures, different depths, and different provenance.
A fine-grained basis (many simple predicates) may require more sequential
layers because each predicate excludes little; a coarse basis (fewer,
more powerful predicates) can reduce depth but each predicate is harder
to evaluate.
(A formal conservation law relating predicate complexity to determination
depth appears in~\cite{hellerstein2026complexity}.)
The transactional section illustrates this concretely: the commit/abort
basis and the scheduling-enriched basis yield different tower structures
for the same workload (Section~\ref{sec:scheduling-basis}).

We impose one restriction on bases throughout this paper:

\begin{definition}[Persistent commitment basis]
  \label{def:persistent}
  A commitment basis $\Phi$ is \emph{persistent} if for every
  $\varphi \in \Phi$ and all histories $H_1 \sqsubseteq H_2$:
  if applying $\varphi$ at $H_1$ excludes an outcome $o$, then
  applying $\varphi$ at $H_2$ also excludes $o$.
  Formally, for all outcomes $o \in O$,
  $
    o \notin \Spec(H_1 \cdot \varphi)
    \Longrightarrow
    o \notin \Spec(H_2 \cdot \varphi).
  $
\end{definition}

\noindent
Persistence is distinct from shrinkage.
Shrinkage is a property of a single commitment event applied at a fixed
history: after applying $\varphi$ at $H_1$, the admissible set does not
re-grow at extensions of $H_1 \cdot \varphi$.
Persistence compares two events with the same predicate applied at
different histories: applying $\varphi$ at a later
$H_2 \sqsupseteq H_1$ must exclude at least the same outcomes as applying
it at $H_1$.
(A ``majority vote'' predicate satisfies shrinkage but not persistence:
at $H_1$ with votes $2{:}A, 1{:}B$ it excludes $B$; at $H_2$ with
$2{:}A, 3{:}B$ it excludes $A$ instead.)

Persistence is the paper's main structural assumption.
It guarantees that determined specifications are history-invariant
(Theorem~\ref{thm:resolution-implies-monotone}), enabling classical
provenance once all commitments have been discharged.
The sealing basis for Datalog with negation, the commit/abort basis for
serializability, and snapshot predicates under snapshot isolation are all
persistent.

Multiple commitments may be applied in sequence.
Because a commitment's effect may depend on which outcomes are jointly
present, the order of application can matter:
$\Spec(H \cdot \varphi \cdot \psi)$ and
$\Spec(H \cdot \psi \cdot \varphi)$ may differ.

\subsection{Determinations}

Individual commitments typically express only partial semantic
commitments.
We therefore model semantic resolution using finite compositions of
commitments, applied as \emph{sequential refinement}.

\begin{definition}[Determination]
  \label{def:determination}%
  A \emph{determination} over a commitment basis $\Phi$ is a finite
  sequence of commitments applied at successive histories:
  $D = \varphi_1(H_0) \cdot \varphi_2(H_1) \cdot \cdots \cdot \varphi_m(H_{m-1})$
  where $\varphi_i \in \Phi$ and $H_i = H_{i-1} \cdot \varphi_i$.
  The result is $(\Spec \mid D)(H_0) \triangleq \Spec(H_m)$.
  The \emph{determination of a history} $D(H)$ is the subsequence of
  commitment events in $H$, listed in the order induced by $\rightarrow$.
\end{definition}

\noindent
Commitment events form a total order within any history (each succeeds all
maximal events of its prefix), so $D(H)$ is always a well-defined sequence.
Because each $\varphi_i$ acts on $\Spec(H_{i-1})$---which depends on all
preceding commitments---the order of application can matter.

\paragraph{Layer-sequencing notation.}
Commitments that commute (can be applied in any order without changing the
result) may be grouped into \emph{layers}.
We use $\seq$ to denote a sequence of such layers:

\begin{definition}[Sequencing of layers (notation)]
  \label{def:layer-seq}
  Let $L_1,\ldots,L_k$ be multisets of commitments.
  We write $L_1 \seq L_2 \seq \cdots \seq L_k$
  for the determination obtained by applying the commitments of $L_1$ (in any
  order), then those of $L_2$, and so on:
  \[
    (\Spec \mid (L_1 \seq \cdots \seq L_k))(H)
    \;\triangleq\;
    \Spec(H \cdot L_1 \cdot L_2 \cdots L_k),
  \]
  where $H \cdot L$ abbreviates appending all commitments in $L$ after $H$ in any
  order.
  This notation is used only to expose layering; it imposes no additional algebraic
  structure beyond commutativity within each layer.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Resolving determination]
  A determination $D$ \emph{resolves} $\Spec$ if $\Spec \mid D$ is determined
  (Definition~\ref{def:determined}): a function from histories to outcomes.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Resolution Enables Provenance}
\label{sec:mono-theorem}

Classical provenance requires a unique outcome at each input: derivations
are traced through a commutative semiring over a fixed, resolved instance.
When $|\Spec(H)| > 1$, this prerequisite fails.
We establish that resolution under a persistent basis both \emph{suffices}
to restore the prerequisite and is \emph{necessary}---no semiring scheme
can operate without it.

\begin{assumption}[Prefix-closure of histories]
  \label{ass:prefix-closed}
  If $H \in \Hist$ and $H' \sqsubseteq H$, then $H' \in \Hist$.
\end{assumption}

\begin{theorem}[Persistence implies history-invariance]
  \label{thm:resolution-implies-monotone}
  Assume prefix-closure (Assumption~\ref{ass:prefix-closed}).
  Let $\Phi$ be a persistent commitment basis and let $D$ be a determination
  over $\Phi$ that resolves $\Spec$.
  Then $(\Spec \mid D)(H)$ is independent of $H$:
  for all $H_1 \sqsubseteq H_2$,
  $(\Spec \mid D)(H_1) = (\Spec \mid D)(H_2)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  Since $D$ resolves $\Spec$, both $(\Spec \mid D)(H_1)$ and
  $(\Spec \mid D)(H_2)$ are singletons; call them $\{o_1\}$ and $\{o_2\}$.
  Write $D = \varphi_1 \cdot \cdots \cdot \varphi_m$.
  Since $(\Spec \mid D)(H_1) = \{o_1\}$, for every $o \neq o_1$ we have
  $o \notin \Spec(H_1 \cdot \varphi_1 \cdots \varphi_m)$.
  Applying persistence inductively along the chain
  ($H_1 \cdot \varphi_1 \cdots \varphi_j \sqsubseteq
  H_2 \cdot \varphi_1 \cdots \varphi_j$ for each $j$),
  we obtain $o \notin \Spec(H_2 \cdot \varphi_1 \cdots \varphi_m)$.
  Hence $o_2 \neq o$ for every $o \neq o_1$, so $o_2 = o_1$.
\end{proof}

\noindent
History-invariance means the determined outcome depends only on the
sequence of commitments, not on where in history they are applied.
We may therefore write $(\Spec \mid D)$ without a history argument
and abbreviate $D = \varphi_1 \cdot \cdots \cdot \varphi_m$, eliding
history annotations.

\begin{theorem}[Necessity: ambiguity blocks semiring provenance]
  \label{thm:necessity}
  Let $|\Spec(H)| \ge 2$ with distinct $o_1, o_2 \in \Spec(H)$ that
  induce distinct database instances (i.e., differ on at least one base
  fact).
  Then for any query $Q$ that reads the differing fact, the semiring
  provenance of some tuple under $Q$ differs between $o_1$ and $o_2$,
  and no single semiring annotation can simultaneously produce both values.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  Let $f$ be a base fact present in the instance induced by $o_1$ but
  absent in that induced by $o_2$.
  Under $o_1$, any query reading $f$ has $\Prov_Q(t \mid o_1)$ with a
  term involving $\alpha(f) \neq 0$.
  Under $o_2$, the same query cannot use $f$ (it is absent), so
  $\Prov_Q(t \mid o_2)$ lacks this term.
  A single annotation $\alpha$ is a function of one instance; it cannot
  produce both values.
\end{proof}

\section{Determinations and Provenance Structure}
\label{sec:conditional-semiring}
This section connects the semantic notion of determination
(Section~\ref{sec:prelim}) to provenance.
Throughout, we fix a specification $\Spec$ over a history class $\Hist$ and
a persistent commitment basis $\Phi$; the set of minimal resolving
determinations is denoted $\mathcal{D}$.

\subsection{Conditioned Provenance}

Fix a commutative semiring $(K, +, \cdot, 0, 1)$ for derivational provenance
(e.g., the polynomial semiring $\mathbb{N}[X]$ of~\cite{green2007provenance}).
For a specification $\Spec$, query $Q$, history $H$, tuple $t$, and a
resolving determination $D \in \mathcal{D}$, the \emph{conditioned
provenance} of $t$ under $D$ is
$\Prov_{Q \mid \Spec \mid D}(t, H) \in K$:
the ordinary semiring provenance of $t$ for query $Q$ computed under the
determined specification $\Spec \mid D$ at history $H$.
When $\Spec$ and $H$ are clear from context, we write simply
$\Prov_{Q \mid D}(t)$.
Conditioned provenance is classical provenance, conditioned on a particular
semantic resolution.

Once $D$ is fixed, $\Spec \mid D$ is single-valued and history-invariant
(Theorem~\ref{thm:resolution-implies-monotone}), so classical semiring
provenance applies without modification.
For depth-$0$ specifications---where no nontrivial commitments are
required---conditioned provenance coincides with classical provenance
directly.

\subsection{The Commitment Tower}
\label{sec:algebra}

Classical algebraic provenance lives in a commutative
semiring~\cite{green2007provenance}.
Determination provenance requires a richer structure---a \emph{commitment
tower}---that decomposes provenance computation into phases: semantic
commitments must be discharged before classical derivational provenance
applies.
The tower is a structural decomposition, not a new algebra; its role is to
identify \emph{when} and \emph{why} classical provenance applies, by making
explicit the semantic prerequisites that must be resolved first.

Consider a determination with $k$ layers,
$L_1 \seq L_2 \seq \cdots \seq L_k$.
The number of layers $k$ is the \emph{determination
depth}---the height of the commitment tower.
Write $\Spec_0 = \Spec$ for the original specification and
$\Spec_i = \Spec \mid (L_1 \seq \cdots \seq L_i)$ for the specification
after discharging the first $i$ layers.

\paragraph{Each layer is a commutative monoid.}
Within layer~$i$, commitments commute: the order of application does not
affect the resulting admissible set.
The commitments in $L_i$ therefore form a commutative monoid $M_i$ under
conjunction, with the trivial commitment (no restriction) as identity.
Which monoid arises at layer~$i$ is conditioned on the layers above:
the carrier of $M_i$---which commitments are valid and which outcomes they
act upon---is determined by the specification prefix $\Spec_{i-1}$.
Discharging $M_i$ (applying all commitments in $L_i$) produces $\Spec_i$,
which in turn determines the monoid $M_{i+1}$.

\paragraph{Layers compose by sequential discharge.}
Between layers, composition is sequential and generally non-commutative:
applying one layer may enable or disable commitments in the next.
The sequencing operator $\seq$ carries no algebraic meaning beyond ordered
refinement---it is the mechanism that transitions from one monoid to the next
by conditioning the specification.

\paragraph{The semiring foundation.}
At the base of the tower ($i = k$), the specification $\Spec_k = \Spec \mid D$
is determined: single-valued and, under a persistent basis, history-invariant
(Theorem~\ref{thm:resolution-implies-monotone}).
All remaining variation is purely derivational.
At this point the algebraic regime shifts: we leave the world of commitment
monoids and enter the world of provenance semirings.
Derivations compose exactly as in classical semiring
provenance~\cite{green2007provenance}---alternative derivations of the same
fact combine by semiring addition, joint use of multiple facts by semiring
multiplication.

\paragraph{Summary.}
A commitment tower is a sequence of commutative monoids
$M_k, \ldots, M_1$ resting on a provenance semiring $K$.
It is a structural decomposition: each monoid represents one layer of
semantic commitment, and discharge proceeds sequentially until the semiring
foundation is exposed.
Classical provenance is the special case of height~$0$.
Per-determination provenance $\Prov_{Q \mid D}(t)$ is the semiring element
released by discharging the tower $D$.
A fuller algebraic treatment---including potential universality properties
and composition laws---is deferred to Appendix~\ref{app:algebra-future}.

\subsection{The Determination Semiring}
\label{sec:det-semiring}

The commitment tower describes the algebraic structure \emph{within} a single
determination.
In many settings, a single determination is the natural object of study:
when explaining what happened in a particular execution, one fixes the
determination that corresponds to that execution and reads off the
derivational provenance at the foundation.
This is the descriptive use of determination provenance---it explains a
specific history under a specific resolution---and it is sufficient for
classical lineage questions.

Some provenance questions, however, require reasoning \emph{across}
determinations.
Would this query result have arisen under a weaker transactional isolation
level?
Would this Datalog conclusion hold under a different semantics for negation?
Is the system overconstrained---producing outcomes that depend on
commitments that could have been relaxed?
These are counterfactual questions about alternative resolutions of the same
history, and answering them requires comparing the provenance of a tuple
across multiple determinations simultaneously.

The resulting algebraic structure is familiar.
Let $\mathcal{D}$ denote the set of minimal resolving determinations for
$\Spec$ over the commitment basis $\Phi$; this set is finite.

\begin{definition}[Determination Provenance]
  \label{def:det-provenance}
  For a specification $\Spec$, query $Q$, history $H$, and tuple $t$, the
  \emph{determination provenance} of $t$ is the function
  \[
    \Prov_{Q \mid \Spec}(t, H) : \mathcal{D} \to K
  \]
  mapping each resolving determination $D \in \mathcal{D}$ to the conditioned
  provenance $\Prov_{Q \mid \Spec \mid D}(t, H)$, with value $0$ when
  $t \notin (\Spec \mid D)(H)$.
  When $\Spec$ and $H$ are clear from context, we write $\Prov_Q(t)$.
\end{definition}

\noindent
Determination provenance is a $K$-relation over
determinations~\cite{green2007provenance}: it records, for each possible
semantic resolution, how the tuple is derived under that resolution.
Conditioned provenance is the special case of evaluating at a single point:
$\Prov_Q(t)(D) = \Prov_{Q \mid D}(t)$.
Classical provenance is the further special case where $|\mathcal{D}| = 1$
(depth~$0$, no commitments needed).

The \emph{support}
$\mathrm{supp}(\Prov_Q(t))
  \triangleq
  \{\, D \in \mathcal{D} \mid \Prov_Q(t)(D) \neq 0 \,\}$
is the set of determinations under which $t$ holds.

These supports form a commutative semiring under the operations induced by
query evaluation:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item When a tuple is derived from two premises jointly (semiring
        multiplication in $K$), its support is contained in the
        \emph{intersection} of the premises' supports: $t$ can hold only
        under determinations that support both premises.
  \item When a tuple has two alternative derivation paths (semiring addition
        in $K$), its support contains the \emph{union} of the paths'
        supports: $t$ holds under any determination that supports either
        path.
\end{itemize}
The resulting structure on supports is the Boolean algebra
$(2^{\mathcal{D}}, \cup, \cap, \emptyset, \mathcal{D})$---a commutative
semiring with $\cup$ as addition and $\cap$ as multiplication.
This is the \emph{determination semiring}: it captures which determinations
support a tuple and how those supports compose through queries.
For instance, in the transactional example of the introduction, values $b$
and $c$ have full support
($\mathrm{supp}(\Prov_Q(b)) = \mathrm{supp}(\Prov_Q(c)) = \mathcal{D}$),
while $d$'s support is a proper subset---it holds only under determinations
in which $T_Q$ commits before $T_3$.
Section~\ref{sec:robustness} applies this structure to formalize robustness
and sensitivity across determinations.

\paragraph{Relationship to Green et al.}
The determination semiring is an instance of the provenance semiring
framework of~\cite{green2007provenance}, with determinations playing the role
of tuple annotations.
Just as Green et al.\ annotate tuples with elements of a semiring $K$ to
track how input tuples contribute to query results, we annotate tuples with
subsets of $\mathcal{D}$ to track which semantic resolutions support them.
The structure $(2^{\mathcal{D}}, \cup, \cap, \emptyset, \mathcal{D})$ is
analogous to their $\mathrm{PosBool}(B)$ semiring for incomplete
databases---positive Boolean formulas with $\vee$ as addition and $\wedge$ as
multiplication---rather than to their why-provenance semiring (in which both
operations coincide).
The distinction matters: in our setting, joint use of two premises
\emph{intersects} their supports (both must hold), while alternative
derivation paths \emph{union} them (either suffices).

\paragraph{The two algebras do not flatten.}
The outer algebra treats determinations as atomic indices; the inner algebra
decomposes each into sequentially discharged layers.
Disjunction ($\cup$) does not distribute over sequencing ($\seq$) because
later layers depend on earlier discharges, so a disjunction of
determinations with different prefixes cannot be decomposed into a single
tower.

\paragraph{Computational cost.}
$|\mathcal{D}|$ may be exponential in the size of the commitment basis.
When all commitments commute (a single-layer tower), supports admit compact
representation as positive Boolean formulas over commitment
variables (Proposition~\ref{prop:collapse-depth-1}).
For deeper towers, compact representation requires respecting inter-layer
validity constraints; this remains open.

\subsection{Filtration of the Determination Semiring}
\label{sec:filtration}

The commitment tower and the determination semiring are not independent
structures: the tower induces a natural \emph{filtration} on the semiring
that connects query-relative depth to algebraic structure.

Two determinations $D, D' \in \mathcal{D}$ \emph{agree at level $k$} if
they apply the same commitments in layers $1, \ldots, k$.
Agreement at level $k$ is an equivalence relation on $\mathcal{D}$;
write $[D]_k$ for the equivalence class of $D$ at level $k$.
These classes refine as $k$ increases:
$[I]_0 = \mathcal{D}$ (vacuous) and $[I]_d = \{I\}$ (full agreement).

\begin{definition}[Level-$k$ support]
  A set $S \subseteq \mathcal{D}$ is a \emph{level-$k$ support} if it is a
  union of level-$k$ equivalence classes.
  Equivalently, $S$ is closed under level-$k$ agreement:
  if $D \in S$ and $D'|_{\le k} = D|_{\le k}$, then $D' \in S$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Filtration]
  Define $\mathcal{F}_k \triangleq \{S \subseteq \mathcal{D} \mid
  S \text{ is a level-}k\text{ support}\}$.
  This yields:
  \[
    \mathcal{F}_0 \;\subseteq\; \mathcal{F}_1 \;\subseteq\; \cdots
    \;\subseteq\; \mathcal{F}_d \;=\; 2^{\mathcal{D}},
  \]
  where $\mathcal{F}_0 = \{\emptyset, \mathcal{D}\}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Filtration respects the semiring]
  \label{prop:filtration-semiring}
  Each $\mathcal{F}_k$ is closed under $\cup$ and $\cap$, hence
  $(\mathcal{F}_k, \cup, \cap, \emptyset, \mathcal{D})$ is a
  sub-semiring of the determination semiring for each $k$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  Unions and intersections of unions-of-equivalence-classes are again
  unions-of-equivalence-classes.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Collapse at depth 1]
  \label{prop:collapse-depth-1}
  When all commitments commute (determination depth $d = 1$), the tower
  is a single commutative monoid, the filtration has exactly two levels
  ($\mathcal{F}_0 = \{\emptyset, \mathcal{D}\}$ and
  $\mathcal{F}_1 = 2^{\mathcal{D}}$), and the determination semiring
  coincides with $\mathrm{PosBool}(\Phi)$---positive Boolean formulas
  over commitment variables, with $\vee$ as addition and $\wedge$ as
  multiplication.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  At depth $1$, all commitments form a single layer.
  Level-$1$ equivalence classes are singletons (each determination is
  uniquely identified by its single-layer content), so
  $\mathcal{F}_1 = 2^{\mathcal{D}}$.
  Each determination corresponds to a conjunction of commitment variables
  (which commitments were applied); supports correspond to disjunctions
  of such conjunctions---exactly $\mathrm{PosBool}(\Phi)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Query-relative depth]
  \label{def:query-depth}
  The \emph{query-relative depth} of a tuple $t$ is
  $\mathrm{qdepth}(t) \triangleq
  \min\{k \mid \mathrm{supp}(\Prov_Q(t)) \in \mathcal{F}_k\}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Characterization]
  \label{prop:qdepth-characterization}
  \begin{enumerate}[nosep,label=(\alph*)]
    \item $\mathrm{qdepth}(t) = 0$ iff $t$ is robust
          ($\mathrm{supp} = \mathcal{D}$) or impossible
          ($\mathrm{supp} = \emptyset$).
    \item $\mathrm{qdepth}(t) = k$ iff all determinations agreeing on
          layers $1, \ldots, k$ agree on whether $t$ holds, but some
          pair agreeing on layers $1, \ldots, k{-}1$ disagrees.
    \item $\mathrm{qdepth}(t) = d$ iff $t$'s presence depends on the
          full determination.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  (a)~$\mathcal{F}_0 = \{\emptyset, \mathcal{D}\}$ by definition.
  (b)~$\mathrm{supp} \in \mathcal{F}_k$ means the support is closed under
  level-$k$ agreement, so layers $k{+}1, \ldots, d$ are irrelevant.
  Minimality of $k$ means some level-$(k{-}1)$ class is split.
  (c)~Immediate from (b).
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Query-relative depth in the transactional setting]
  \label{ex:qdepth-txn}
  In Example~\ref{ex:example}, the tower has depth~$1$ (all commit
  decisions commute when no cycles exist).
  Value $b$ has $\mathrm{qdepth}(b) = 0$: it holds under every
  determination (robust).
  Value $d$ has $\mathrm{qdepth}(d) = 1$: its support is a proper subset
  of $\mathcal{D}$ (the determinations where $T_Q$ commits before $T_3$),
  which is not in $\mathcal{F}_0 = \{\emptyset, \mathcal{D}\}$.
  Classical provenance suffices for $b$; determination provenance is needed
  for $d$.
\end{example}

\begin{corollary}[Query evaluation respects the filtration]
  \label{cor:propagation-filtration}
  Under positive relational algebra:
  $\mathrm{qdepth}(t_1 \bowtie t_2) \le
  \max(\mathrm{qdepth}(t_1), \mathrm{qdepth}(t_2))$,
  and likewise for union.
  Query evaluation cannot increase query-relative depth beyond the maximum
  depth of its inputs.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
  Join computes $\mathrm{supp}(t_1) \cap \mathrm{supp}(t_2)$;
  union computes $\mathrm{supp}(t_1) \cup \mathrm{supp}(t_2)$.
  By Proposition~\ref{prop:filtration-semiring}, both remain in
  $\mathcal{F}_{\max(k_1, k_2)}$.
\end{proof}

\noindent
The filtration says the tower is reflected \emph{inside} the semiring as a
graded sub-semiring structure.
Robust tuples live at grade~$0$ (classical provenance); fully contingent
tuples at grade~$d$ (full tower required).
A system answering queries at depth $\le k$ need only retain the first $k$
layers---the formal basis for \emph{log parsimony}
(Appendix~\ref{app:certificates}).

\begin{remark}[Dependence on layering]
  \label{rem:layering-dependence}
  The filtration depends on the canonical layering of the tower (the Foata
  normal form: commitments grouped into maximal commuting batches).
  Different valid orderings \emph{within} a layer do not affect the
  equivalence classes, since intra-layer commitments commute by definition.
  The filtration is therefore well-defined up to the choice of layer
  boundaries, which are determined by the commutation structure.
\end{remark}

\section{Transactions as Semantic Ambiguity}
\label{sec:transactions}
% ================================================================

We now instantiate the framework in transactional systems, where ambiguity
arises from concurrency: multiple consistent executions may be admissible for
the same observed history.
We formalize the commitment basis, characterize determination depth across
isolation levels, and show how determination provenance distinguishes robust
from contingent query results.

\subsection{Transactional Histories}

\begin{definition}[Transactional history]
  \label{def:txn-history}
  A \emph{transactional history} is a history $H = (E, \rightarrow)$ whose
  events include:
  \begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item $\mathsf{begin}(T_i)$, $\mathsf{commit}(T_i)$, $\mathsf{abort}(T_i)$
          --- lifecycle events for transaction $T_i$;
    \item $\mathsf{r}(T_i, x, v)$, $\mathsf{w}(T_i, x, v)$ --- read and write
          operations by $T_i$ on object $x$ with value $v$.
  \end{itemize}
  We assume transactions arrive asynchronously, commit requests are handled
  individually and immediately (not batched), and read/write operations are
  subject to non-deterministic interleaving across concurrent transactions.
  Aborted transactions and their operations remain visible in the history;
  they are not erased.
  Commit events are retained in the history: they finalize conflict edges
  (an edge $T_i \to T_j$ is present only when both transactions' conflicting
  operations have occurred and both have committed) and record the ordering
  decisions that constitute the determination.
\end{definition}

\noindent
The retention of aborted transactions in the history is essential: an abort is
a commitment event that resolved a conflict, and determination provenance must
record it.
Commit events also serve as commitment events: committing $T_i$ irrevocably
finalizes $T_i$'s position in the serial order and removes its edges from
the active conflict graph.
The ordering among conflicting operations is established by the interleaving
of reads and writes during execution; the commit makes that ordering
irrevocable by finalizing $T_i$'s effects.
The conflict edge $T_i \to T_j$ (recording that $T_i$'s conflicting
operation preceded $T_j$'s) becomes a permanent fact in the history once
$T_i$ commits.

\begin{definition}[Conflict]
  \label{def:conflict}
  Two operations from distinct transactions $T_i \neq T_j$ \emph{conflict} if
  they access the same object $x$ and at least one is a write.
  Following Adya~\cite{adya1999weak}, we distinguish three conflict types:
  \emph{write-write} ($\mathsf{ww}$: both write $x$),
  \emph{write-read} ($\mathsf{wr}$: $T_i$ writes $x$, $T_j$ reads $x$), and
  \emph{read-write} ($\mathsf{rw}$: $T_i$ reads $x$, $T_j$ writes $x$;
  also called an \emph{anti-dependency}).
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Conflict graph]
  \label{def:conflict-graph}
  The \emph{conflict graph} $G(H)$ of a transactional history $H$ is the
  directed graph whose vertices are the \emph{active} (uncommitted,
  non-aborted) transactions in $H$ and whose edges represent conflicts:
  $T_i \to T_j$ if $T_i$ and $T_j$ conflict and $T_i$'s conflicting
  operation precedes $T_j$'s in $H$.
  When $T_i$ commits, all edges adjacent to $T_i$ are removed from the
  active conflict graph: $T_i$'s position in the serial order is finalized.
  When $T_i$ aborts, all edges adjacent to $T_i$ are likewise removed
  (its operations are rolled back).
\end{definition}

\subsection{Isolation Levels as Specifications}

Following Adya~\cite{adya1999weak}, each isolation level is characterized by
which cycles in the conflict graph are forbidden.
The specification maps a history to the set of outcomes (committed database
states) consistent with the isolation level's acyclicity constraint.

\begin{definition}[Isolation-level specification]
  \label{def:isolation-spec}
  For an isolation level $L$, the specification $\Spec_L$ is an invariant
  on the active conflict graph: at every point in the history, the conflict
  graph over running (uncommitted, non-aborted) transactions, restricted to
  the edge types relevant to $L$, must satisfy $L$'s acyclicity
  constraint~\cite{adya1999weak}.
  An outcome (the sequence of commit and abort decisions) is admissible if
  this invariant is maintained throughout the history.
  Stronger isolation levels forbid more edge types in cycles; weaker levels
  permit more.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Transactional histories as conflict-graph streams]
  \label{rem:history-as-stream}
  The transactional history in our framework is not a traditional transaction
  schedule (a linear interleaving of operations).
  It is a stream of conflict-graph states---or equivalently, deltas to the
  active conflict graph---punctuated by commit and abort events that remove
  vertices and their adjacent edges.
  Each read or write event may add an edge; each commit or abort removes a
  vertex.
  The specification's acyclicity constraint is an invariant over this evolving
  graph.
\end{remark}

\noindent
We consider three levels:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Read committed (RC):} forbids only $\mathsf{wr}$ cycles
        involving uncommitted writes (dirty reads).
        No constraint on committed conflict graphs.
  \item \textbf{Serializability (SER):} forbids all cycles in the full
        conflict graph ($\mathsf{ww} + \mathsf{wr} + \mathsf{rw}$).
  \item \textbf{Snapshot isolation (SI):} multiversion; each transaction reads
        from a snapshot.  Forbids $\mathsf{ww}$ conflicts between concurrent
        transactions (first-committer-wins) and certain $\mathsf{rw}$
        cycles~\cite{adya1999weak}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Commitment Basis}

The interleaving of read and write operations is determined by the
environment: operations arrive non-deterministically and are serviced
immediately.
The conflict edges that result are therefore \emph{environment events}---they
are observed, not chosen.
The system's only irrevocable decisions are \emph{commit} and \emph{abort}:

\begin{definition}[Commit commitment]
  \label{def:commit-commitment}
  The commitment $\varphi_{\mathsf{commit}(T_i)}$ irrevocably finalizes
  $T_i$'s operations: $T_i$'s effects become durable and $T_i$ is removed
  from the active conflict graph (along with all its adjacent edges).
  This commitment is valid only if, after removing $T_i$, the acyclicity
  invariant of the isolation level is not violated---that is, committing
  $T_i$ does not leave a forbidden cycle among the remaining active
  transactions.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Abort commitment]
  \label{def:abort-commitment}
  The commitment $\varphi_{\mathsf{abort}(T_i)}$ irrevocably discards
  $T_i$'s operations: $T_i$'s effects are rolled back and $T_i$ is removed
  from the active conflict graph.
  Abort is always valid (a transaction may always be aborted).
\end{definition}

\noindent
The commitment basis is thus $\Phi = \{\varphi_{\mathsf{commit}(T_i)},
\varphi_{\mathsf{abort}(T_i)} \mid T_i \text{ active}\}$.
Ordering predicates are not commitments---they are environment facts
recorded in the history as conflict edges accumulate.

\begin{proposition}[Commutativity of commit/abort on independent transactions]
  \label{prop:commit-commute}
  Commit and abort commitments on transactions that share no conflict edges
  commute: their joint effect on the active conflict graph and the admissible
  set is independent of application order.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  If $T_i$ and $T_j$ share no edges, removing one does not affect the
  other's adjacency or the validity of committing/aborting the other.
  The resulting conflict graph is the same regardless of order.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Non-commutativity under shared conflicts]
  \label{rem:commit-noncommute}
  When $T_i$ and $T_j$ participate in a common cycle, committing one may
  make committing the other invalid (the remaining cycle would violate the
  acyclicity invariant), while aborting one may make committing the other
  valid (the cycle is broken).
  In such cases, commit/abort decisions do not commute, and the
  determination has depth $> 1$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Determination Depth by Isolation Level}

\begin{proposition}[Read committed: depth 0]
  \label{prop:rc-depth-zero}
  Under read committed, $\Spec_{\mathit{RC}}$ is future-monotone:
  extending a history with new operations and commit/abort events can only
  add new visible values, never retract previously visible ones.
  Hence no commitments are needed and determination depth is~$0$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  Under RC, a transaction $T_Q$ may read any value written by a
  committed transaction.
  New commit events can only add committed writes to the set of values
  visible to future readers.
  No previously visible value becomes invisible: committed writes persist.
  Hence for every $o \in \Obs(H)$ and every extension $H' \sqsupseteq H$,
  there exists $o' \in \Obs(H')$ with $o \Ord o'$ (where $\Ord$ is set
  inclusion on visible values).
  The specification is future-monotone.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Serializability: depth from conflict structure]
  \label{prop:ser-depth}
  Under serializability with the commit/abort commitment basis:
  \begin{enumerate}[nosep,label=(\alph*)]
    \item When the active conflict graph is acyclic, every active transaction
          can be committed independently.  All commit decisions commute
          (Proposition~\ref{prop:commit-commute}, since no cycles exist),
          giving depth~$1$.
    \item When cycles exist and are pairwise disjoint (share no transactions),
          each cycle can be broken independently by a single abort, giving
          depth~$1$.
    \item When cycles overlap (share transactions), commit/abort decisions
          may not commute, and depth is at least~$2$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
  (a)~With no cycles, committing any transaction preserves acyclicity
  (removing a vertex from an acyclic graph leaves it acyclic).
  All commit decisions are independently valid and act on disjoint vertices,
  so they commute.
  (b)~Disjoint cycles share no transactions, so aborting one transaction per
  cycle breaks all cycles independently.
  The abort decisions act on disjoint vertices and therefore commute.
  (c)~See Example~\ref{ex:overlapping-cycles}: aborting $T_1$ leaves Cycle~2
  intact, requiring a second abort whose validity depends on the first
  decision.
  Committing $T_3$ (rather than aborting it) would leave both cycles intact;
  aborting $T_3$ breaks both.
  The choice for $T_3$ affects which subsequent decisions are valid, so the
  decisions do not commute.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Overlapping cycles and depth]
  \label{ex:overlapping-cycles}
  Consider five transactions with two conflict cycles sharing $T_3$:
  $T_1 \to T_2 \to T_3 \to T_1$ (Cycle~1) and
  $T_3 \to T_4 \to T_5 \to T_3$ (Cycle~2).
  If the system broke Cycle~1 by aborting $T_1$,
  $T_3$ survived and Cycle~2 remained---requiring a second abort.
  The second abort depended on which transactions survived the first:
  the two decisions could not be made simultaneously.
  Hence the determination has depth~$\ge 2$.
  Had the system instead aborted $T_3$, both cycles would have broken in a
  single decision (depth~$1$).
  The determination records which resolution occurred.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}[Snapshot isolation: depth from snapshot dependencies]
  \label{prop:si-depth}
  Under snapshot isolation, the commitment basis includes snapshot
  commitments $\varphi_{\snap(T_i) = U}$ in addition to ordering and abort
  commitments.
  Snapshot commitments do not commute in general: the first-committer-wins
  (FCW) rule can render a snapshot choice for $T_j$ infeasible once a
  conflicting assignment for $T_i$ is fixed.
  Worst-case determination depth under SI is $O(n)$ where $n$ is the number
  of transactions, arising from chains of FCW dependencies.
\end{proposition}

\subsection{Running Example Revisited}

Returning to Example~\ref{ex:example}: $T_2$, $T_3$, and $T_Q$ conflict on
$(2,d)$ with no cycles.
Two representative determinations:
$D_{\mathsf{in}} \triangleq
  \varphi_{\mathsf{commit}(T_2)} \cdot \varphi_{\mathsf{commit}(T_Q)}
  \cdot \varphi_{\mathsf{commit}(T_3)}$
yields $\Prov_{Q \mid D_{\mathsf{in}}}(d)=x_2$;
$D_{\mathsf{out}} \triangleq
  \varphi_{\mathsf{commit}(T_2)} \cdot \varphi_{\mathsf{commit}(T_3)}
  \cdot \varphi_{\mathsf{commit}(T_Q)}$
yields $d$ absent.
Prior to resolution, even the \emph{form} of the provenance question for $d$
is ambiguous (why vs.\ why-not).
Values $b$ has full support
($\mathrm{supp}(\Prov_Q(b)) = \mathcal{D}$)
and is robust; $d$ is contingent.

\subsection{Complexity of Robustness}

\begin{theorem}[Robustness under serializability is coNP-complete]
  \label{thm:robustness-conp}
  Given a transactional history $H$ with conflict graph $G$, a conjunctive
  query $Q$, and a tuple $t$: deciding whether $t$ is robust (present in
  $Q$'s result under every resolving determination) is coNP-complete.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  \emph{Membership in coNP.}
  To refute robustness, guess a resolving determination $D$ (a set of
  commit/abort decisions that makes $G$ acyclic): guess which transactions
  to abort, verify acyclicity of the remaining conflict graph in polynomial
  time, compute the resulting database state, evaluate $Q$, and check that
  $t$ is absent.
  This is a polynomial-time verifiable certificate for non-robustness.

  \emph{coNP-hardness.}
  We reduce from \textsc{Validity} (given a DNF formula $\psi$ over
  variables $x_1, \ldots, x_n$, is $\psi$ true under every truth
  assignment?), which is coNP-complete.
  Given $\psi$, construct a transactional history as follows.
  %
  For each variable $x_i$, create two transactions:
  $T_i^+$ writes value $\mathsf{true}$ to object $o_i$, and
  $T_i^-$ writes value $\mathsf{false}$ to object $o_i$.
  Both $T_i^+$ and $T_i^-$ have committed; their write-write conflict on
  $o_i$ creates an edge in $G$.
  Under last-writer-wins semantics, the commit order determines the final
  value of $o_i$: if $T_i^+$ is ordered after $T_i^-$, then
  $o_i = \mathsf{true}$ (representing $x_i = \mathsf{true}$), and vice
  versa.
  No cycles arise (each pair conflicts only on its own object), so no
  aborts are needed; every ordering of each pair is a valid determination.
  %
  For each term $t_j = \ell_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \ell_m$ in $\psi$,
  define a conjunctive query clause that checks whether the corresponding
  objects have the values matching the literals.
  The overall query $Q$ returns a distinguished tuple $t^*$ iff at least
  one term is satisfied (the disjunction of all term-queries).

  Under any resolving determination, the commit ordering of each pair
  $(T_i^+, T_i^-)$ induces a truth assignment to $x_i$.
  The tuple $t^*$ is present iff the induced assignment satisfies $\psi$.
  Hence $t^*$ is robust (present under every determination) iff $\psi$ is
  valid (true under every assignment).
\end{proof}

\noindent
The result complements the algebraic propagation (Corollary~\ref{cor:propagation-filtration}):
supports compose cleanly through queries, but \emph{deciding} whether a
support equals $\mathcal{D}$ (full robustness) is computationally hard.

\subsection{Snapshot Isolation}

Snapshot isolation (SI) is multiversion: each transaction reads from a
snapshot.
The commitment basis adds snapshot predicates:

\begin{definition}[Snapshot commitment (SI only)]
  \label{def:snapshot-commitment}
  $\varphi_{\snap(T_i) = U}$ records that $T_i$
  read from a snapshot containing exactly the effects of transactions in $U$.
\end{definition}

\noindent
The first-committer-wins (FCW) rule constrains which snapshot
assignments are jointly admissible.
Snapshot commitments do not commute in general (FCW can render
a choice for $T_j$ infeasible once $T_i$'s is fixed),
introducing non-commutativity beyond serializability and
potentially deepening the tower even for acyclic conflict graphs.
Worst-case depth under SI is $O(n)$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:si-depth}).

\subsection{Enriching the Basis: Scheduling as Commitment}
\label{sec:scheduling-basis}

The commit/abort basis treats operation interleaving as environment.
When a system actively \emph{schedules} conflicting operations, those
ordering decisions are commitments:

\begin{definition}[Ordering commitment]
  \label{def:ordering-commitment}
  $\varphi_{T_i \prec T_j}$ records that the system chose to execute
  $T_i$'s conflicting operation before $T_j$'s, excluding outcomes with
  the reverse order.
\end{definition}

\noindent
The enriched basis is
$\Phi' = \Phi \cup \{\varphi_{T_i \prec T_j} \mid T_i, T_j \text{ conflict}\}$.
Ordering commitments on distinct edges commute.
The boundary between environment and commitment is a \emph{modeling choice}
reflecting system architecture:
under pure OCC, interleaving is environment;
under an active scheduler, it is commitment;
under partial control (e.g., hot keys serialized, cold keys uncontrolled),
the determination records which orderings were chosen and which were observed.

\paragraph{Connection to batch-optimal scheduling.}
Baccaert et al.~\cite{baccaert2026makespan} show that finding a
conflict-serializable schedule of minimal makespan is NP-complete;
Cheng et al.~\cite{cheng2024scheduling} give practical heuristics.
In our framework, a batch-optimal scheduler produces a single-layer
determination
$D_{\mathsf{sched}} = \{\varphi_{T_i \prec T_j} \mid

of commuting ordering commitments (depth~$1$).
The NP-completeness of computing this layer illustrates a general
tradeoff: determination depth can be traded for computational complexity
within a single layer.
The enriched basis extends beyond batch scheduling to online arrival
(ordering decisions made incrementally, potentially non-commuting),
partial knowledge (access sets unknown until execution), and mixed control.

\section{Change and Robustness Across Determinations}
\label{sec:robustness}
% ================================================================

Determination provenance records not only how outcomes are derived, but the
semantic commitments under which those derivations are valid.
This section formalizes two such families of questions and grounds them in the
examples developed earlier.

% \subsection{Intra- vs.\ Inter-Specification Questions}

We distinguish two classes of provenance questions.
\emph{Intra-specification} questions fix a specification $\Spec$ and range over its
resolving determinations, asking which outcomes hold under different
admissible resolutions of the \emph{same} specification.
\emph{Inter-specification} questions compare outcomes across distinct
specifications $\Spec$ and $\Spec'$, asking which outcomes persist when the
specification itself changes—for example, when a transactional system moves from
serializability to snapshot isolation, or when a logic program adopts a different
negation semantics.
Both question families are expressed using determination provenance; they simply
quantify over different spaces.

\subsection{Intra-Specification Queries: Robustness}

Robustness is an intra-specification property.
Fix a specification $\Spec$ and the set $\mathcal{D}$ of resolving determinations.
An outcome $o$ is \emph{robust} with respect to $\mathcal{D}$ iff
$\mathrm{supp}(\Prov_Q(o)) = \mathcal{D}$---equivalently,
$\forall D \in \mathcal{D}.\; o \in (\Spec \mid D)(H)$.
In the determination semiring, robustness
is the multiplicative identity: the tuple's support is the full set
$\mathcal{D}$.
In Example~\ref{ex:example}, $b$ is robust; $d$ is contingent.

\subsection{Inter-Specification Queries: Preservation under Semantic Change}
\label{sec:semantic-change}

Semantic change is an inter-specification question: it compares which outcomes
are admitted under different specifications for the same history.

\paragraph{Preservation.}
Given specifications $\Spec$ and $\Spec'$, outcome $o$, and
sets $\mathcal{D}$ and $\mathcal{D}'$ of all resolving determinations
for $\Spec$ and $\Spec'$ respectively,
$o$ is \emph{preserved} from $\Spec$ to $\Spec'$ iff
\[
  \forall D \in \mathcal{D},\;
  \bigl(o \in (\Spec \mid D)(H)\bigr)
  \;\Rightarrow\;
  \exists D' \in \mathcal{D}'.\;
  \bigl(o \in (\Spec' \mid D')(H)\bigr).
\]

\paragraph{Serializable outcomes under snapshot isolation.}
Snapshot isolation admits a larger space of determinations than serializability.
Preservation therefore reduces to the following question:
\emph{given an SI determination, does there exist a serializable determination
  that admits the same outcome?}

Determination provenance provides concrete syntax for this question.
An SI determination records snapshot choices and commit-admissibility constraints.
From these predicates, one can check whether the induced visibility and conflict
relations admit a topological sort constituting a serial execution.
If so, the outcome is preserved when strengthening from SI to serializability;
otherwise it is inherently non-serializable.
Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-concurrency} works through a detailed example.

This reframes preservation as a \emph{semantic validation problem} over
determinations rather than as a comparison of executions.
Mixed-consistency execution policies—e.g., allowing SI but ignoring
any non-serializable outcome rows—can therefore be expressed and enforced directly in
terms of determination provenance, without re-executing the workload.

\paragraph{Summary.}
Robustness identifies conclusions invariant across determinations;
preservation compares conclusions across specifications.
Determination provenance provides a common framework for both.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% ================================================================

\noindent\textbf{Data provenance and explanations.}
Algebraic provenance provides a uniform framework for explaining query
conclusions under fixed, resolved semantics by annotating derivations with
elements of a commutative semiring
\cite{green2007provenance,suciu2011probabilistic,dalvi2012probabilistic,olteanu2015factorized,senellart2018provenance}.
A related body of work studies explanations and causality for query answers,
including why-not provenance, causal responsibility, and counterfactual
explanations
\cite{buneman2001why,halpern2001causes,meliou2010causality,glavic2015survey}.

From the perspective of this paper, these approaches operate at
determination depth~0: semantic ambiguity has already been resolved, and
provenance explains derivations relative to a fixed semantics.
This includes work on incremental view maintenance and \emph{homeostasis},
which identifies queries whose answers are invariant under admissible input
changes and therefore require no additional semantic commitments to maintain
\cite{gehrke2002homeostasis}.
In our framework, homeostasis corresponds precisely to robustness at
determination depth~0: the query answer is invariant across all admissible
outcomes of the specification, so no commitment is needed to justify it.
Determination provenance generalizes this by explaining which commitments are
required when invariance fails, and how classical provenance depends on those
commitments once resolution has occurred.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Negation and non-monotonic semantics.}
Classical semantics for Datalog with negation provide well-understood mechanisms
for resolving semantic ambiguity, with established relationships under syntactic
restrictions.
We do not propose new semantics.
Instead, Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-datalog} uses Datalog with negation as a controlled
setting in which resolution strategies are explicit and formal, making it
possible to study how different semantic commitments induce distinct
determination structures and provenance behavior.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Transactional and systems provenance.}
Provenance has been studied in transactional, workflow, and database
settings under an assumed execution or isolation level
\cite{bernstein1987concurrency,adya1999weak,cerone2015consistency,cheney2009provenance,freire2008provenance}.
In these settings, semantic resolution is implicit: provenance explains results
relative to a chosen execution, snapshot, or schedule.
From our perspective, such approaches again operate at determination depth~0.

A distinct body of systems work studies execution provenance or systems lineage,
recording causal dependencies among processes, messages, and events to explain
observed behavior
\cite{seltzer2005provenance,bates2015trustworthy,sigelman2010dapper}.
These approaches take a maximalist view of provenance, capturing entire execution
histories to account for ambiguity in scheduling and message interleaving.
Determination provenance provides a semantic foundation for structuring such
systems provenance: rather than committing to a single reconstructed execution,
it makes explicit the semantic commitments required for a diagnostic conclusion
to hold across admissible executions, supporting principled notions of parsimony
and robustness.
Recent systems work on mixed isolation regimes, such as Milano et al.'s MixT
\cite{milano2020mixt}, addresses related concerns operationally; our contribution
is a semantic and explanatory account that makes such mixtures explicit and
checkable.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Determination complexity.}
A companion paper~\cite{hellerstein2026complexity} develops a complexity
theory for determinations, characterizing the cost and depth of semantic
resolution.
The present paper focuses on explanation rather than complexity:
determinations are assumed given, and determination provenance explains how
observations depend on them.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ================================================================

This paper reframes provenance around a simple observation: many modern systems do
not produce a single semantic outcome from a history, but a space of admissible
ones.
In such settings, explaining an observation requires accounting for how ambiguity
was resolved—not merely how derivations proceeded after the fact.
Determination provenance makes those semantic commitments explicit and
composable, recovering classical algebraic provenance once resolution is fixed
while retaining visibility into the assumptions that made resolution possible.

Our development shows that this perspective is not domain-specific.
In transactional systems, determination provenance explains how query
observations depend on ordering and isolation commitments.
In logic programming with negation, it exposes the semantic dependencies that
govern resolution.
Across domains, certificates provide a minimal interface for carrying forward
exactly the information required to support further reasoning.

Determination provenance also opens a broader research agenda.

\paragraph{Robustness and semantic sensitivity.}
Once observations are annotated with the determinations under which they hold, it
becomes natural to ask which are robust across determinations and which are
fragile.
This enables provenance questions about semantic stability, policy change, and
compatibility across regimes that lie beyond traditional why- and how-provenance.
Developing principled robustness measures—qualitative or quantitative—remains an
open direction.

\paragraph{Beyond databases.}
Semantic ambiguity and resolution arise in consistency models, coordination
protocols, access-control systems, and machine learning pipelines.
Determination provenance suggests a unifying foundation for provenance in
ambiguous computational systems.

\paragraph{Reversible commitments.}
The main structural assumption of this paper is \emph{persistence}: once a
commitment excludes an outcome, it remains excluded at all history extensions.
This is what guarantees history-monotonicity of determined specifications
(Theorem~\ref{thm:resolution-implies-monotone}) and enables classical
provenance at the foundation of the commitment tower.
All examples in this paper satisfy persistence naturally.
However, many practical settings involve commitments that can be
reversed---transactional rollback, speculative execution, backtracking
search---where persistence fails.
Extending the framework to reversible commitments (a group rather than a
monoid at each layer) and characterizing the resulting provenance structure
is an open direction; a companion paper~\cite{hellerstein2026complexity}
discusses related structural questions.

The success of algebraic provenance lay in identifying the right semantic object
to explain.
Determination provenance extends that insight: when meaning itself depends on
resolution, provenance must explain the assumptions that made meaning possible.

\section{Open Questions: Tower Structure}
\label{app:open-questions}
% ================================================================

The commitment tower is defined as the layered normal form of a resolving
determination: commitments grouped into maximal commuting batches, discharged
sequentially.
Several structural questions about this object remain open.

\paragraph{The tower as Foata normal form.}
In classical trace theory~\cite{mazurkiewicz1977concurrent}, the Foata normal
form canonically represents a word in a partially commutative monoid as a
sequence of maximal independent layers.
The commitment tower is the analog for determinations---but with a crucial
difference: commutation is \emph{dynamic} (history-contingent) rather than
static.
Whether $\varphi$ and $\psi$ commute depends on the current admissible set,
which changes as earlier layers are discharged.
A formal characterization of the tower as a dynamic Foata normal form---and
conditions under which two determinations are equivalent (produce the same
determined specification)---would give the tower a precise algebraic identity
beyond its definition.

\paragraph{Tower height under history extension.}
Under a persistent basis, the admissible set can only shrink as history grows.
Fewer admissible outcomes means fewer commitments are needed and fewer
opportunities for non-commutativity.
\emph{Conjecture:} for a persistent basis, the tower height (determination
depth) is monotonically non-increasing under history extension.
If true, this would mean that as more information arrives, resolution becomes
easier (or at least no harder)---a natural property that connects to the
Coordination Criterion's characterization of depth-$0$ specifications as
those requiring no commitments at all.

\paragraph{Query-relative tower height.}
A provenance query $Q$ may depend on only a subset of the tower's layers.
If $Q$'s answer is determined after discharging $k < d$ layers (the remaining
layers resolve ambiguity irrelevant to $Q$), the effective tower for $Q$ has
height~$k$.
This connects to layer certificates (Appendix~\ref{app:certificates}):
a certificate for layer~$i$ is sufficient for $Q$ precisely when $Q$ does not
depend on finer distinctions within that layer.
Characterizing query-relative tower height---which queries require which
layers---would provide a formal basis for adaptive log retention.

\paragraph{Reversible commitments.}
The persistent-basis assumption guarantees that the tower discharges
irreversibly.
In settings with rollback (transactional abort-and-retry, speculative
execution, backtracking search), commitments can be undone.
Extending the tower to reversible commitments---replacing monoids with
groups at each layer---and characterizing the resulting provenance structure
is open.

\paragraph{Propagation of determination provenance through queries.}
Green et al.\ showed that semiring provenance propagates through positive
relational algebra: annotations compose via $+$ (union) and $\times$ (join).
The determination semiring $(2^{\mathcal{D}}, \cup, \cap)$ should propagate
analogously: join intersects supports (both premises must hold), union takes
their union (either suffices), and projection/selection preserve supports.
A formal propagation theorem---stating that determination provenance is
computed by evaluating the query over the determination semiring---would
precisely parallel Green et al.'s foundational result at the new level.

\paragraph{Complexity of robustness.}
Deciding whether a tuple is robust under serializability (present under
\emph{every} resolving determination) appears to be coNP-complete.
The upper bound is clear: to refute robustness, guess an abort set (a
feedback vertex set of the conflict graph), verify acyclicity, evaluate the
query, and check that the tuple is absent---an NP certificate for
non-robustness.
A hardness reduction (likely from tautology or a variant of ``is this tuple
in every minimal model?'') would establish that robustness checking is
inherently intractable, complementing the algebraic propagation result:
propagation is compositional, but deciding full support is hard.

\section{Appendix: The Commitment Tower---Algebraic Details}
\label{app:algebra}
% ================================================================

This appendix gives precise structural content to the commitment tower
introduced in Section~\ref{sec:algebra}.
The goal is to situate
determination provenance precisely relative to existing algebraic frameworks
and to justify the claim that classical semiring provenance applies once the
tower has been fully discharged.

\subsection{Sequential Discharge Versus Algebraic Combination}

Commitments across layers compose by sequential discharge.
Given a specification $\Spec$ and commitments $\varphi_1,\varphi_2$,
\[
  \Spec(H \cdot \varphi_1 \cdot \varphi_2)
\]
need not equal
\[
  \Spec(H \cdot \varphi_2 \cdot \varphi_1),
\]
because commitments may be ambiguity-sensitive.
Accordingly, the inter-layer sequencing operator $\seq$ is not an algebraic
operation: it is the mechanism that transitions from one monoid to
the next by discharging the current layer and conditioning the specification
for the layer below.

This distinction is essential.
Commitments \emph{restrict} the space of admissible outcomes; they do not
\emph{combine} explanations.
Algebraic combination of explanations occurs only at the semiring foundation,
after all commitment layers have been discharged.

\subsection{Commutative Monoids Within a Layer}

Within a single layer, commitments commute by construction.
Fix a specification prefix $\Spec^{\ell}$ obtained after discharging all
layers above layer~$\ell$.
Let $L$ be the set of commitments in layer~$\ell$.

For $\varphi,\psi \in L$,
\[
  \Spec^{\ell}(H \cdot \varphi \cdot \psi)
  \;=\;
  \Spec^{\ell}(H \cdot \psi \cdot \varphi).
\]
Thus commitments in $L$ form a commutative monoid under conjunction, with the
trivial commitment (no restriction) as identity.

This monoid is \emph{conditional}: its carrier---which commitments are valid
and what effect they have---depends on the determination prefix.
No global algebra over commitments is assumed or required.
Discharging the monoid (applying all commitments in $L$) produces the
specification prefix for the next layer.

\subsection{The Semiring Foundation}

The key algebraic transition occurs when the last commitment layer is
discharged.

\begin{proposition}[Determined specifications admit semiring provenance]
  \label{prop:semiring}
  If $\Spec \mid D$ is determined and the commitment basis is persistent
  (Definition~\ref{def:persistent}), then derivational provenance over
  $\Spec \mid D$ is captured by a commutative semiring.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
  By Theorem~\ref{thm:resolution-implies-monotone}, $\Spec \mid D$ is
  history-invariant.
  Because it is also single-valued (determined), $(\Spec \mid D)(H)$ is a singleton
  for every history $H$; no further commitments can eliminate outcomes, so semantic
  conditioning has ceased.
  Because it is monotone, the unique outcome grows monotonically with
  the history: $H_1 \sqsubseteq H_2$ implies that every fact derivable at $H_1$
  remains derivable at $H_2$.
  Under these two conditions, derivations compose exactly as in monotone query
  evaluation over a fixed instance: alternative derivations of the same fact
  combine by semiring addition, and joint use of multiple facts combines by
  semiring multiplication.
  This is precisely the algebraic regime established
  by Green et al.~\cite{green2007provenance} for positive relational queries.
  Although $\Spec \mid D$ need not syntactically be a Datalog program, it is
  algebraically equivalent, for provenance purposes, to monotone evaluation over
  a fixed instance, which suffices.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The Tower as a Whole}

A commitment tower of height~$k$ over a provenance semiring $K$ consists of:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item commutative monoids $M_k, M_{k-1}, \ldots, M_1$, where
        each $M_i$ is conditioned on the specification prefix obtained by
        discharging $M_k, \ldots, M_{i+1}$; and
  \item a commutative semiring $K$ at the foundation, exposed once all
        monoids have been discharged.
\end{enumerate}
Layers are numbered top-down: $M_k$ is the first layer discharged, $M_1$ the
last.
Classical provenance is the degenerate case $k = 0$: no commitment layers,
just the semiring.

Determination provenance recovers the algebraic conditions required for
semiring provenance by explicitly discharging semantic ambiguity through the
tower.
Seen this way, the commitment tower is a semantic prelude to algebraic
provenance: it explains \emph{when} and \emph{why} classical provenance
applies, rather than assuming a fixed monotone semantics from the outset.

\section{Toward an Algebraic Theory of Commitment Towers}
\label{app:algebra-future}
% ================================================================

The commitment tower as presented in this paper is a structural decomposition:
it identifies the phases of provenance computation (commitment discharge
followed by derivational evaluation) without claiming a new algebraic object
with universal properties.
Several directions for algebraic development remain open.

\paragraph{Collapse at depth 1.}
When all commitments commute (determination depth~$1$), the tower reduces to a
single commutative monoid tensored with the provenance semiring.
In this case, the determination semiring coincides with
$\mathrm{PosBool}(B)$~\cite{green2007provenance}, where $B$ is the set of
commitment variables.
A formal proof of this collapse and characterization of the resulting structure
is straightforward but deferred.

\paragraph{Universality.}
Green et al.\ showed that $\mathbb{N}[X]$ is the most general provenance
semiring for positive relational algebra: every other semiring annotation is a
homomorphic image.
An analogous question for the commitment tower would ask: is there a ``most
general'' tower from which all others are homomorphic images?
The conditioning between layers (each monoid depends on the discharge of the
previous) makes this question non-trivial, as the tower is not a fixed algebraic
structure but a \emph{dependent} one.

\paragraph{Composition laws.}
When two determinations share a common prefix, their towers share initial layers.
A formal treatment of tower morphisms---how towers relate under prefix extension,
restriction, and substitution of commitment layers---would enable compositional
reasoning about determination provenance across sub-specifications.

These directions are left for future work.

\section{Layer Certificates}
\label{app:certificates}
% ================================================================

This appendix gives the formal development of layer certificates, summarized
in the body text.
Fix a specification $\Spec$ and a determination
$D=\varphi_1 \cdot \cdots \cdot \varphi_k$ with layering $L_1,\ldots,L_k$.

A \emph{provenance query} is a function $q:(\Spec\mid D)\to A$,
where $A$ is an answer domain (e.g., Boolean, explanations, dependency sets).

\begin{definition}[Certificate]
  Let $\varphi$ be a commitment.
  A certificate for $\varphi$, written $\varphi\models C$, is a predicate
  $C:O\to\{\mathsf{true},\mathsf{false}\}$ such that for all histories $H$,
  $\Spec(H \cdot \varphi)\subseteq\{\,o\in \Spec(H)\mid C(o)\,\}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Sufficient certificate]
  Write $\Spec_i \triangleq \Spec \mid (\varphi_1 \cdot \cdots \cdot \varphi_i)$
  and $J \triangleq \varphi_{i+1} \cdot \cdots \cdot \varphi_k$.
  Let $\chi_{C_i}$ denote the commitment induced by certificate $C_i$:
  appending $\chi_{C_i}$ restricts the admissible set to
  $\{\,o \in \Spec(H) \mid C_i(o)\,\}$.
  $C_i$ is \emph{sufficient for $(J,\mathcal{Q})$} if for all $H$ and all
  $q\in\mathcal{Q}$,
  $(\Spec_i\mid J)(H)=((\Spec\mid\chi_{C_i})\mid J)(H)$ and
  $q((\Spec_i\mid J)(H)) = q(((\Spec\mid\chi_{C_i})\mid J)(H))$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Layer certificate]
  A \emph{layer certificate} is a certificate $C_i$ for layer $L_i$ that is
  sufficient for $(J,\mathcal{Q})$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Query Monotonicity]
  If $\mathcal{Q}_1\subseteq\mathcal{Q}_2$, then every certificate sufficient
  for $(J,\mathcal{Q}_2)$ is sufficient for $(J,\mathcal{Q}_1)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
  Sufficiency for $(J,\mathcal{Q}_2)$ requires preserving resolution and all
  queries in $\mathcal{Q}_2$.
  Since $\mathcal{Q}_1 \subseteq \mathcal{Q}_2$, the same certificate
  preserves all queries in $\mathcal{Q}_1$ a fortiori.
\end{proof}


\section[Datalog with Negation: Worked Determination Provenance]{Datalog$^{\neg}$ Worked Determination Provenance}
\label{sec:appendix-datalog}

This appendix instantiates determination provenance for the Datalog-with-negation
sandbox.
We work through the running example below under stratified, well-founded, and stable
semantics, making explicit the commitments, their dependency structure,
and the resulting provenance annotations.
The purpose is to justify the determination shapes and depths for Datalog
negation (see also Figure~\ref{fig:det-tables} for the transactional cases).

\begin{example}[Datalog with negation]
  \label{ex:datalog-negation}
  Consider the following program $P$ over EDB facts in a history $H$.
  For notational economy we use a fixed constant $c$ and take all predicates unary, so the EDB fact is $a(c)$ and the derived atoms are $p(c),q(c),r(c),s(c)$.
  \begin{align*}
    p(c) & \leftarrow a(c).      & r(c) & \leftarrow \neg s(c). \\[1mm]
    q(c) & \leftarrow \neg p(c). & s(c) & \leftarrow \neg r(c).
  \end{align*}
  Assume $H$ contains the single extensional fact $a(c)$.
  The pair $(P,H)$ contains both a stratified fragment
  ($q(c)$ depends negatively on $p(c)$, and $p(c)$ depends positively on $a(c)$)
  and a cycle through negation ($r \leftrightarrow \neg s$).
  We use this single instance to exhibit, side-by-side, three distinct structures
  of determination provenance.
\end{example}

Throughout, we use \emph{sealing predicates}
$\varphi_{\seal}(X)$, which commit that the set of atoms $X$ is complete:
no additional atoms in $X$ will be derived in any admissible outcome.
Such predicates rule out outcomes and therefore constitute genuine semantic
commitments.

\subsection{Stratified Semantics}

\paragraph{Determination structure.}
In stratified semantics, predicates are partitioned into strata
$S_1,\dots,S_k$ such that negation flows strictly from higher to lower strata.
Resolving ambiguity requires committing that each stratum has stabilized before
negation may be applied in the next stratum.

For the running example (Example~\ref{ex:datalog-negation}), the stratified fragment
consists of the predicates defining $p(c)$ and $q(c)$.
Resolution therefore requires sealing the lower stratum containing $p(c)$.

\paragraph{Resolving determination.}
A resolving determination for the stratified fragment is
\[
  D_{\mathrm{strat}}
  \;\triangleq\;
  \varphi_{\seal}(S_1)
  \seq
  \varphi_{\seal}(S_2)
  \seq \cdots \seq
  \varphi_{\seal}(S_k),
\]
% [added \seq]
where each $S_i$ is the set of atoms whose truth is fixed at stratum~$i$.
These sealing predicates do \emph{not} commute: sealing $S_i$ is a prerequisite
for applying negation commitments in $S_{i+1}$.
Accordingly, the determination depth is exactly $k$, as recorded in
the stratified case.

\paragraph{Provenance.}
Once $D_{\mathrm{strat}}$ is fixed, the specification is fully resolved.
Derivational provenance applies without further semantic refinement.
For example,
\[
  \Prov_{\mid D_{\mathrm{strat}}}(p(c)) = \Prov(a(c)).
\]
Absences such as $\neg q(c)$ are derivational consequences of the sealed lower stratum
and require no additional commitments.

\subsection{Well-Founded Semantics}

\paragraph{Determination structure.}
Well-founded semantics (WFS) applies to programs with negation cycles.
It resolves ambiguity by alternating between positive derivation and the elimination
of \emph{unfounded sets}, yielding a three-valued outcome domain
$\{\mathbf{t},\mathbf{f},\mathbf{u}\}$.

Crucially, WFS still relies on the same stratified-prefix commitments as stratified
semantics: the lower strata must be sealed before negation is interpreted.
Beyond this prefix, resolution proceeds through an \emph{alternating sequence} of
additional sealing commitments that classify atoms as determined or undefined.

\paragraph{Resolving determination.}
A resolving determination under WFS can be written schematically as
\[
  D_{\mathrm{wfs}}
  \;\triangleq\;
  \varphi_{\seal}(S_1)
  \seq \cdots \seq
  \varphi_{\seal}(S_k)
  \seq
  \varphi_{\seal}(U_1)
  \seq
  \varphi_{\seal}(U_2)
  \seq \cdots,
\]
% [added \seq]
where each $U_i$ is an unfounded set eliminated at alternation~$i$.
These commitments are non-commuting and may require an unbounded number of
alternations.
Hence WFS has no fixed finite determination depth in general, as indicated in
the well-founded case.

For the running example, the limit of this process yields the unique partial
outcome
\[
  p(c)=\mathbf{t},\quad q(c)=\mathbf{f},\quad r(c)=\mathbf{u},\quad s(c)=\mathbf{u}.
\]

\paragraph{Provenance.}
Because WFS yields a single resolved outcome (in the lifted domain), there is no
branching among determinations.
Once $D_{\mathrm{wfs}}$ is fixed, provenance is again depth~0:
\[
  \Prov_{\mid D_{\mathrm{wfs}}}(p(c)) = \Prov(a(c)).
\]
The difference from stratified semantics lies not in multiplicity of outcomes, but
in the unbounded, alternating structure of the determination itself.

\subsection{Stable Model Semantics}

\paragraph{Determination structure.}
Stable semantics insists on total two-valued models.
Like WFS, it requires sealing the stratified prefix before negation is determined.
Unlike WFS, it does not lift the outcome domain; instead it resolves cycles by
branching among incompatible global hypotheses.

\paragraph{Resolving determinations.}
For the running example, resolution begins with the same stratified-prefix sealing
\[
  \varphi_{\seal}(S_1)
  \seq \cdots \seq
  \varphi_{\seal}(S_k),
\]
% [added \seq]
After this prefix, the negation cycle admits two incompatible resolutions:
\[
  D^{(r)}
  \;\triangleq\;
  \bigl(
  \varphi_{\seal}(S_1)
  \seq \cdots \seq
  \varphi_{\seal}(S_k)
  \bigr)
  \seq
  (r(c)=\mathbf{t},\,s(c)=\mathbf{f}),
\]
\[
  D^{(s)}
  \;\triangleq\;
  \bigl(
  \varphi_{\seal}(S_1)
  \seq \cdots \seq
  \varphi_{\seal}(S_k)
  \bigr)
  \seq
  (s(c)=\mathbf{t},\,r(c)=\mathbf{f}).
\]
% [added \seq]

Within either determination, the remaining commitments are logically simultaneous
and commute.
Thus the determination depth is $k{+}1$: the $k$ stratification stages plus one
additional, non-commuting branching stage.
This is reflected in the stable case.

\paragraph{Provenance.}
Determination provenance must record which stable determination is assumed:
\[
  \Prov_{\mid D^{(r)}}(r(c)) = 1,
  \qquad
  \Prov_{\mid D^{(s)}}(s(c)) = 1.
\]
Only after this choice does ordinary derivational provenance apply.

\subsubsection{Semantic Change Across Negation Semantics}

Holding the program and extensional history fixed, different negation semantics
induce different spaces of resolving determinations.

For example, consider the program
\[
  p(c)\leftarrow a(c),\qquad
  q(c)\leftarrow \neg r(c),\qquad
  r(c)\leftarrow \neg q(c),
\]
with $H=\{a(c)\}$.
Under WFS, there is a unique resolving determination with
$q(c)=r(c)=\mathbf{u}$.
Under stable semantics, there are two incompatible determinations, one with
$q(c)=\mathbf{t}$ and one with $r(c)=\mathbf{t}$.

The conclusion $p(c)$ is preserved across semantics, while $q(c)$ and $r(c)$ are not.
Determination provenance makes this preservation (and non-preservation) explicit
and checkable.

\subsection{Certificates and Semantic-Change Queries}

Fix a specification $\Spec$ and a resolving determination $D$.
A \emph{provenance query} is a function
\[
  q : (\Spec \mid D) \to A,
\]
where $A$ is an answer domain (e.g., Boolean, explanations, dependency sets),
including classical why/why-not provenance and the semantic-change queries of
Section~\ref{sec:robustness}.
Such queries depend only on the restrictions that a determination imposes on
admissible outcomes, not on the full resolution process.

Commitments restrict admissible outcomes; a \emph{certificate}
summarizes these restrictions.
Formally, a certificate is a sound over-approximation: it may admit outcomes that
a full determination would exclude, but must exclude none that a determination
admits.
This lossiness is acceptable precisely because provenance queries are evaluated
only up to the distinctions they are designed to observe.

In the negation sandbox, a natural certificate for the stratified prefix is given
by a \emph{sealed set} of atoms.
Equivalently, this can be represented by a \emph{history antichain}: a frontier in
the partial order of derivations that fixes which facts are considered complete.
In a totally ordered execution, a single timestamp freezes the past; in a
partially ordered history, an antichain plays the same role, sealing exactly the
downward closure of a sub-history without imposing an artificial total order.

Under a fixed resolving determination, a minimal certificate sufficient for
derivational provenance of $p(c)$ need only preserve the sealing commitments for
the stratified prefix.
By contrast, answering a semantic-change query such as
``Would $q(c)$ become true under stable semantics?''
requires a strictly stronger certificate: it must preserve the unresolved
negation structure itself, not merely one resolution.

This illustrates the query-parametric nature of certificate sufficiency
(Appendix~\ref{app:certificates}): supporting richer provenance questions strictly
increases the semantic information that must be retained.

\section{Transactional Worked Determination Provenance}
\label{sec:appendix-concurrency}
% ===============================================================

This appendix instantiates determination provenance for the transactional sandbox
used in Section~\ref{sec:transactions}.
As in the Datalog appendix, we make explicit the commitments,
their dependency structure, and the resulting provenance annotations.
The goal is to justify the determination shapes and depths summarized in
Figure~\ref{fig:det-tables}.

Throughout, commitments rule out admissible executions and therefore
constitute genuine semantic commitments.
Once a resolving determination is fixed, the specification becomes single-valued
and provenance reduces to ordinary derivational provenance.

% ===============================================================
\subsection{Serializability}
% ===============================================================

\paragraph{Determination structure.}
Under conflict serializability, admissible outcomes are those whose conflict
graph is acyclic.
At runtime, the system's commit and abort decisions resolve ambiguity.
When the system also controls scheduling (Section~\ref{sec:scheduling-basis}),
ordering commitments $\varphi_{T_i \prec T_j}$ record which interleaving was
chosen; otherwise, the interleaving is an environment fact and only
commit/abort decisions are commitments.
This appendix uses the enriched (scheduling) basis for expository clarity.

For the running example below, the conflict graph is acyclic---no aborts were
needed---so the determination consists entirely of ordering commitments
(scheduling decisions that determined the conflict edges).
\paragraph{Data, query, and history.}
Let $S(k,y)$ be a relation and consider the query
$Q(y) \drule S(k,y)$.
The database is initially empty.
Five transactions execute:
\[
  \begin{array}{ll}
    T_1: \textsf{insert } S(1,b) \ (x_1) \qquad &
    T_2: \textsf{insert } S(2,b) \ (x_2)          \\
    T_3: \textsf{insert } S(3,c) \ (x_3) \qquad &
    T_4: \textsf{insert } S(4,d) \ (x_4)          \\
    T_5: \textsf{delete } S(4,d).               &
  \end{array}
\]
A query transaction $T_Q$ evaluates $Q$.

The history alone does not determine whether $d$ is visible to $T_Q$; this depends
on the relative ordering of $T_4$, $T_5$, and $T_Q$.

\paragraph{Resolving determinations.}
Two valid resolving determinations under serializability are:
\[
  D_{\mathsf{in}} \triangleq
  T_1 \prec T_2 \prec T_3 \prec T_4 \prec T_Q \prec T_5,
  \qquad
  D_{\mathsf{out}} \triangleq
  T_1 \prec T_2 \prec T_3 \prec T_4 \prec T_5 \prec T_Q.
\]
Both are conjunctions of ordering predicates and therefore arise in a single
semantic stage.

\paragraph{Provenance.}
Once a resolving determination is fixed, provenance is purely derivational.
Conditioned on $D_{\mathsf{in}}$, the resolved snapshot contains $b,c,d$:
\[
  \Prov_{Q \mid D_{\mathsf{in}}}(b)=x_1+x_2,\quad
  \Prov_{Q \mid D_{\mathsf{in}}}(c)=x_3,\quad
  \Prov_{Q \mid D_{\mathsf{in}}}(d)=x_4.
\]
Conditioned on $D_{\mathsf{out}}$, tuple $d$ is absent and classical why-not
provenance applies.

Determination provenance records the semantic assumption under which the
derivation holds; e.g.,
\[
  \Prov_{Q \mid D_{\mathsf{in}}}(d) = x_4.
\]

\paragraph{Same observation, different derivational provenance.}
Even when multiple serializations yield the same observation, they need not agree
on derivational provenance.
Adding
$T_{-2}:\ \textsf{delete } S(2,b)$
yields executions in which $b$ is observed with provenance $x_1$ or $x_1+x_2$,
depending on the serialization.
Thus determination provenance distinguishes semantic explanations even when the
observable outcome is unchanged.

% ===============================================================
\subsection{Snapshot Isolation}
\label{sec:appendix-SI}
% ===============================================================

\paragraph{Determination structure.}
As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:transactions}, all isolation levels share the
same basic enforcement pattern (orient conflict edges, abort to break forbidden
cycles), but differ in their commitment bases.
Snapshot isolation (SI) is multiversion: each transaction reads from a specific
committed state, so the determination must additionally record \emph{which
snapshot each transaction observed}.

The SI commitment basis thus includes:
(i) ordering and abort commitments (as in serializability), plus
(ii) snapshot predicates $\snap(T)=U$, committing that $T$ reads from the
effects of exactly the transactions in $U$, and
(iii) a global \emph{first-committer-wins} predicate $\varphi_{\FCW}$ that
constrains which snapshot assignments are jointly admissible.

Snapshot commitments do not commute in general: the FCW rule can render a
snapshot choice for $T_j$ infeasible once a conflicting assignment for $T_i$
is fixed.
This introduces non-commutativity beyond what serializability requires.

\paragraph{Extended history.}
In addition to $T_1$–$T_5$, assume the database initially contains $S(5,e)$ and
$S(6,f)$, and the following transactions execute concurrently:
\[
  \begin{array}{ll}
    T_6: \textsf{read } S(5,e);\ \textsf{delete } S(6,f) \qquad &
    T_7: \textsf{read } S(6,f);\ \textsf{delete } S(5,e).
  \end{array}
\]
A query transaction $T_Q$ again evaluates $Q$.

\paragraph{Resolving determination.}
One admissible SI determination is:
\[
  \begin{aligned}
    D_{\mathrm{SI}} \triangleq {} &
    \snap(T_1)=\snap(T_2)=\snap(T_3)=\snap(T_4)=\snap(T_6)=\snap(T_7)=\emptyset \\
                                  & \seq\; \snap(T_5)=\{T_4\}                   \\
                                  & \seq\; \snap(T_Q)=\{T_1,\dots,T_7\}         \\
                                  & \seq\; \varphi_{\FCW}.
  \end{aligned}
\]
% [added \seq]

\paragraph{Outcome and provenance.}
Under $D_{\mathrm{SI}}$, the final state contains $b$ and $c$, but not $d,e,f$.
The query result is $\{b,c\}$ with derivational provenance:
\[
  \Prov_{Q \mid D_{\mathrm{SI}}}(b)=x_1+x_2,\qquad
  \Prov_{Q \mid D_{\mathrm{SI}}}(c)=x_3.
\]

\paragraph{Semantic change and serializability.}
Although $D_{\mathrm{SI}}$ is admissible under snapshot isolation, it does not admit
any serial execution.
The concurrent writes of $T_6$ and $T_7$ produce the classical write-skew anomaly:
a dependency cycle that cannot be linearized into a total order.

Determination provenance makes this explicit.
Restricting attention to SI determinations whose snapshot predicates admit a
topological sort recovers exactly the serializable outcomes.

\paragraph{Determination depth.}
Unlike serializability, which resolves ambiguity in a single commuting stage,
snapshot isolation may require an independent snapshot commitment for each
transaction.
Thus SI has worst-case determination depth $O(|H|)$, as recorded in
Figure~\ref{fig:det-tables}.
Once an SI determination is fixed, provenance again reduces to depth--0
derivational provenance.

% ===============================================================
\subsection{Certificates and Semantic-Change Queries}
% ===============================================================

As in the negation sandbox, provenance queries depend only on the restrictions that
a determination imposes on admissible outcomes.
A \emph{certificate} summarizes these restrictions and may safely over-approximate
the determination.

Under serializability, a certificate need only record a set of ordering predicates
sufficient to justify the observation.
Under snapshot isolation, however, answering semantic-change queries—such as
whether an SI outcome admits a serial explanation—requires preserving the snapshot
structure itself, not merely a particular resolved state.

As in Section~\ref{sec:appendix-datalog}, certificates are therefore
query-parametric.
Richer provenance questions require retaining strictly more semantic information,
reflecting the deeper and less-commutative structure of the underlying
determination.

\section{Appendix: Determination Provenance for Systems Lineage}
\label{app:systems-lineage}
% ================================================================

This appendix illustrates determination provenance in a systems setting that
lies outside traditional database semantics.
The example is adapted from the distributed tracing model introduced by
Sigelman et al.~\cite{sigelman2010dapper}, abstracting its trace semantics rather
than its implementation details.

The example serves two purposes.
First, it shows that determination provenance extends the database tradition of
provenance to forms of \emph{systems lineage} that have historically resisted
formal treatment.
Second, it demonstrates how the parsimony of determinations---as minimal semantic
commitments---can refine systems lineage explanations by making explicit which
assumptions are required to justify diagnostic conclusions.

Unlike the previous appendices, this setting does not admit a fixed canonical
determination depth.
Semantic commitments may be staged or bundled depending on the diagnostic query,
but determination provenance still cleanly separates observable evidence from
the assumptions required to explain it.

\paragraph{Scope.}
The treatment below is deliberately illustrative rather than fully formal.
The Datalog and transactional sandboxes admit precise commitment bases because
their semantics are mathematically specified; distributed tracing, by contrast,
involves physical clocks and operational assumptions that resist clean
axiomatization.
We therefore describe commitments semi-formally, showing how the determination
provenance framework applies in principle while deferring a complete
formalization to future work on specific tracing systems.

\subsection{Trace Specification and Semantic Ambiguity}

A distributed trace consists of a set of \emph{spans}, each representing an
invocation of a service.
Each span $X$ has a client-side send and receive event
$(\mathsf{cs}_X,\mathsf{cr}_X)$ and a server-side receive and send event
$(\mathsf{sr}_X,\mathsf{ss}_X)$.
Client and server events occur on different hosts with unsynchronized clocks.

The trace specification $\Spec$ maps a history $H$ of timestamped span events to a
set of admissible executions $O$.
An execution assigns each span a concrete placement on a global timeline and
induces a partial order over spans.
The specification enforces only causal constraints:
\[
  \mathsf{cs}_X \prec \mathsf{sr}_X
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  \mathsf{ss}_X \prec \mathsf{cr}_X ,
\]
together with parent--child relationships recorded in the trace metadata.

Because clocks are unsynchronized, these constraints determine only \emph{bounds}
on server-side timestamps.
As a result, multiple executions---with different relative orderings and overlaps
of spans---are admissible under the same observed history.
Diagnostic conclusions such as critical-path analysis are therefore semantically
ambiguous prior to determination.

\subsection{Diagnostic Query: Critical Path}

We consider the diagnostic query:
\begin{quote}
  \emph{Which span lies on the critical path of the request?}
\end{quote}
Formally, the query maps an execution to the span (or set of spans) whose duration
determines the end-to-end latency of the root span.

Under a fixed execution, this query is deterministic.
Under the specification $\Spec$, however, different admissible executions may
yield different critical paths.
Without additional semantic commitments, the query is ill-posed.

\subsection{Commitments}

Determination resolves this ambiguity by committing to semantic assumptions that
fix a particular execution.
Relevant commitments include:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\varphi_{\mathrm{align}(h_i,h_j)}$, committing to a concrete clock
        alignment between hosts $h_i$ and $h_j$ within admissible bounds;
  \item $\varphi_{\mathrm{order}(X,Y)}$, committing that span $X$ precedes span
        $Y$ when their relative order is otherwise unconstrained;
  \item $\varphi_{\mathrm{cp}=X}$, committing that $X$ is the critical-path
        witness \emph{given the preceding alignment and ordering commitments}.
\end{itemize}

These predicates are ambiguity-sensitive and semantically dependent.
For example, committing to a critical-path witness presupposes commitments that
fix the relative placement of competing spans.

\subsection{Resolving Determinations}

Let $B$ and $C$ be sibling spans whose relative order is not determined by causal
constraints.
Two resolving determinations suffice to illustrate the provenance structure:
\[
  I_B \;\triangleq\;
  \varphi_{\mathrm{align}} \cdot \varphi_{\mathrm{order}(B,C)} \cdot
  \varphi_{\mathrm{cp}=B},
\]
\[
  I_C \;\triangleq\;
  \varphi_{\mathrm{align}} \cdot \varphi_{\mathrm{order}(C,B)} \cdot
  \varphi_{\mathrm{cp}=C}.
\]
Both determinations resolve $\Spec$, but they induce different critical-path
outcomes.

\subsection{Determination Provenance}

Once a determination is fixed, critical-path computation is ordinary.
Let $k_B$ (resp.\ $k_C$) denote the derivational justification that $B$ (resp.\ $C$)
is the longest-duration span in the resolved execution.
Determination provenance records:
\[
  \Prov_{\mid I_B}(\mathrm{cp}=B) = k_B,
  \qquad
  \Prov_{\mid I_C}(\mathrm{cp}=C) = k_C .
\]

The provenance makes explicit that the diagnostic conclusion depends on semantic
commitments about clock alignment and span ordering.
Without fixing a determination, the question ``which span is on the critical
path?'' has no determinate answer.

\subsection{Certificates and Parsimony}

Notably, the full trace history is unnecessary to justify the diagnosis.
A sufficient certificate consists of:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the parent--child span structure;
  \item the inequality constraints induced by send/receive events;
  \item the specific alignment and ordering commitments recorded in $I_B$ or
        $I_C$.
\end{itemize}

As in the previous appendices, the certificate acts as a \emph{semantic frontier}:
it freezes exactly those commitments needed to justify the diagnostic observation,
without reconstructing a total execution.
In a totally ordered execution, a timestamp suffices to delimit the past; in a
partially ordered trace, an antichain of events plays the same role, sealing the
relevant downward closure without imposing an artificial linearization.

Determination provenance therefore supports a compressed form of systems lineage:
only the semantic commitments required for the diagnostic conclusion need be
retained.
This refines existing tracing practice by separating observable evidence from
the semantic assumptions used to explain it.

\paragraph{Summary.}
This appendix demonstrates that determination provenance applies naturally to
systems diagnostics.
It extends provenance beyond data derivations to explanations of execution-level
properties, while retaining a minimal, explicit account of the semantic
assumptions required to justify those explanations.

\section{Absence Under Semantic Ambiguity}
\label{app:whynot}
In settings with semantic ambiguity, absence admits distinctions that cannot be
expressed at determination depth~0.

Given a specification $\Spec$ and history $H$, a conclusion may be absent in
several semantically distinct ways:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Contingent absence.}
    The conclusion fails under a particular resolved determination but holds
    under others.
    In the transactional example, tuple $d$ is absent under
    $D_{\mathsf{out}}$ (where $T_5 \prec T_Q$) but present under
    $D_{\mathsf{in}}$ (where $T_Q \prec T_5$).
    Classical why-not provenance under $D_{\mathsf{out}}$ explains the
    derivational failure; determination provenance additionally records that
    the absence is contingent on the ordering commitment.

    In the negation example (Example~\ref{ex:datalog-negation}), atom $s(c)$
    is absent under stable determination $D^{(r)}$ because $r(c)$ is true.
    But $r(c)$ is true only because of the commitment to the $r$-model;
    under $D^{(s)}$, $s(c)$ is true and $r(c)$ is absent.
    The why-not explanation for $s(c)$ under $D^{(r)}$ is
    $\Prov_{\mid D^{(r)}}(\neg s(c))$,
    recording that the absence depends on a semantic commitment, not merely
    on a blocked derivation.

  \item \textbf{Undefinedness (well-founded).}
    Under well-founded semantics, a conclusion may be neither present nor
    absent but \emph{undefined}: the determination does not resolve the
    relevant cycle.
    In the running example, both $r(c)$ and $s(c)$ receive the value
    $\mathbf{u}$ under the well-founded determination.
    Classical why-not provenance has no clean answer here---the atom is not
    absent due to a derivational failure, but because the commitment tower
    is incomplete: the well-founded determination does not discharge the
    layer needed to resolve the $r/s$ cycle.
    This case has no transactional analog (serializable executions are
    always total) and is specific to semantic regimes that admit partial
    resolution.

  \item \textbf{Robust absence.}
    The conclusion fails under \emph{all} resolving determinations of the
    fixed specification $\Spec$.
    In the transactional example, a tuple $(e)$ that was never inserted fails
    under every serialization.
    This is a robust absence: no ordering commitment can make it appear.
    Determination provenance certifies this by showing that the outcome
    lies outside $(\Spec \mid D)(H)$ for every resolving $D$.

    In the negation example, $q(c)$ is robustly absent: $p(c)$ holds under
    every semantics (stratified, well-founded, and stable), so the rule
    $q(c) \leftarrow \neg p(c)$ fails under every determination.
    No semantic commitment can make $q(c)$ true.

  \item \textbf{Inter-specification absence.}
    The conclusion holds under some determinations of a \emph{different}
    specification $\Spec'$ but under none of the determinations admitted by
    $\Spec$ itself.
    For instance, a write-skew outcome visible under snapshot isolation may be
    absent under every serializable determination.
    In the negation setting, an atom that holds under stable semantics but
    not under well-founded semantics (e.g., $r(c)$, which is true in one
    stable model but undefined under WFS) exhibits the same pattern when
    the two semantics are viewed as distinct specifications.
    This is an inter-specification absence, handled via the preservation
    analysis of Section~\ref{sec:robustness} rather than by determination
    provenance alone.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
Classical why-not provenance collapses these cases, because it conditions on a
single resolved semantics and a fixed specification.
Determination provenance makes the first three cases explicit by recording the
semantic commitments under which outcomes hold, explaining absence in terms
of the non-existence, incompleteness, or variability of supporting
determinations rather than in terms of blocked derivations alone.

The taxonomy above classifies \emph{what kind} of absence holds.
The commitment tower provides a natural decomposition of the counterfactual
reasoning needed to explain each case: contingent absence involves semantic
counterfactuals (what if a different determination had been chosen?) composed
with derivational counterfactuals (what if a missing premise had been
present?); undefinedness reflects an incomplete tower; robust absence involves
only derivational counterfactuals; and inter-specification absence requires
changing the specification itself.
