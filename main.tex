\begin{abstract}
Algebraic provenance explains query results by tracing derivations under a fixed,
resolved semantics.
Many modern systems, however, operate under intrinsically ambiguous semantics:
logic programs with negation admit multiple models, transactional systems admit
multiple consistent executions, and policy-aware or probabilistic systems admit
multiple semantic interpretations.
In such settings, it is not enough to explain \emph{what} follows; we must explain
\emph{under which semantic commitments it follows}.

This paper introduces \emph{provenance of interpretations}: a framework for
explaining semantic resolution itself.
Building on a prior complexity theory of interpretation, we show how interpretations
engineer quotients on ambiguous outcome spaces, yielding resolved semantic spaces
where algebraic provenance applies.
We identify a canonical algebraic normal form for interpretation, called a
\emph{conditional semiring}, and introduce \emph{layer certificates} as sufficient
statistics for interpretation provenance.
We develop the framework using Datalog with negation as a semantic sandbox and
transactions as a representative systems setting, clarifying when and how classical
semiring provenance is recovered after resolution.  
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Algebraic provenance is one of the major success stories of database theory.
By representing query results as elements of a commutative semiring—most famously
as provenance polynomials—researchers have obtained a uniform account of
\emph{why}- and \emph{how}-provenance, explanations, uncertainty propagation, and
query optimization across a wide range of query languages
\cite{green2007provenance,suciu2011probabilistic}.
Once query semantics are single-valued and monotone, provenance reduces to
arithmetic.

Yet this success rests on a strong and usually implicit assumption:
that the semantics of interest are already \emph{resolved}.
For each input, there is a single semantic outcome (or a single equivalence class
of outcomes) to explain.
In many of today’s most important semantic settings, this assumption does not
hold.

Many settings are more complex, operating under \emph{intrinsically ambiguous} semantics.
Logic programs with negation admit multiple models.
Transactional systems admit multiple consistent executions.
Distributed systems admit multiple interpretations of failure, membership, or
ordering.
Policy-aware and probabilistic systems admit multiple semantic regimes or possible
worlds.
In all of these cases, the semantic object of interest is not a single outcome,
but a \emph{set} of admissible outcomes.
Classical provenance explains conclusions \emph{within} a fixed semantics, but has
little to say about how that semantics was chosen.

\paragraph{Thesis.}
This paper argues that the missing foundation is not a new provenance algebra, but
a new provenance target.
We introduce \emph{provenance of interpretations}: a framework for explaining
\emph{which semantic commitments were relied upon} in order to reach a conclusion.

We model semantic resolution as a process of \emph{interpretation}: the introduction
of structured semantic commitments that restrict an ambiguous specification to a
more refined one.
Each interpretation induces a \emph{quotient} on the space of admissible outcomes,
collapsing ambiguity into a resolved semantic space.
Algebraic provenance applies precisely in these induced quotient spaces.
Thus, algebra is not lost in ambiguous settings—it is \emph{recovered} through
interpretation.

\medskip

This work builds on a prior complexity framework for interpretation
introduced in~\cite{hellerstein2026interpretation}.
That work formalizes the \emph{cost} of interpretation (how many semantic commitments
are introduced) and its \emph{depth} (the irreducible dependency structure among
those commitments), and studies the computational consequences of deep
interpretations.
In contrast, the focus of this paper is semantic and algebraic:
we show how interpretations engineer quotients that restore algebraic structure,
and how provenance can explain this resolution process itself.

\medskip

\paragraph{Interpretation as quotient engineering.}
Interpretations act by progressively restricting the space of admissible outcomes.
Each semantic commitment rules out outcomes incompatible with it; critically,
whether a commitment is applicable may depend on which outcomes remain.
This dependency structure induces a quotient on the outcome space: outcomes ruled
equivalent by the interpretation are identified, and incompatible outcomes are
eliminated.
When the resulting quotient is resolved, classical algebraic provenance applies.

Different interpretations induce different quotients.
Some collapse ambiguity cheaply; others require deep chains of dependent
commitments.
We characterize interpretations along two orthogonal dimensions:
\emph{cost}, measuring the volume of semantic commitment, and
\emph{depth}, measuring semantic dependency.
Depth, rather than cost, determines when resolution can be parallelized, deferred,
or avoided.

\paragraph{Conditional semirings and certificates.}
A central technical contribution of this paper is to show that interpretations admit
a canonical algebraic normal form, which we call a \emph{conditional semiring}.
Intuitively, interpretation proceeds as a conjunction of conditional refinement
steps, each of which supports algebraic reasoning within its scope.
These layers form a stack of semirings, with full semiring provenance recovered only
after sufficient resolution.

To make interpretation provenance practical, we introduce \emph{layer certificates}:
compact, sufficient summaries of the semantic commitments introduced at each layer.
Certificates need not preserve all information about lower layers—only what is
required to justify conclusions above.
This separation enables minimal, compositional provenance of interpretation.

\paragraph{Representative domains.}
We develop the framework using two representative domains.
We use Datalog with negation as a canonical example of \emph{semantic ambiguity},
where multiple models arise from underspecified inference.
We use transactions as a canonical example of \emph{systems ambiguity}, where
multiple executions or consistency interpretations are admissible.
Our goal is not to re-litigate these domains, but to show that both exhibit the same
foundational gap: provenance that explains not just \emph{what} follows, but
\emph{under which semantic assumptions it follows}.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
This paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item We formalize \emph{provenance of interpretations}, explaining semantic
        resolution itself rather than derivations under a fixed semantics.
  \item We show how interpretations engineer quotients on ambiguous outcome spaces,
        and how algebraic provenance applies precisely in the induced quotients.
  \item We introduce \emph{conditional semirings} as a canonical algebraic normal
        form for interpretation.
  \item We define \emph{layer certificates} as sufficient statistics for
        interpretation provenance, separating algebraic structure from information
        requirements.
  \item We develop the framework in Datalog with negation and sketch its application
        to transactional systems, illustrating a new provenance regime for modern
        systems.
\end{itemize}

\section{Preliminaries: Ambiguous Semantics and Refinement}
\label{sec:prelim}

This section recalls the core semantic constructs from our earlier work on
interpretation complexity~\cite{hellerstein2026interpretation}, adapted here as
background.
We present them in a streamlined form, emphasizing semantic ambiguity,
conditioning, and dependency, while deferring complexity measures and algebraic
structure to later sections.

Our goal is to characterize what it means for a specification to admit multiple
admissible meanings, and to identify the minimal semantic structure that such
ambiguity already entails.
We intentionally defer any discussion of how ambiguity is resolved, measured, or
explained until subsequent sections.

\subsection{Histories}

We model partial information using histories in the sense of Lamport, but in the
weakest possible form.

\begin{definition}[History]
A \emph{history} is a finite partially ordered set
\[
  H = (E, \rightarrow),
\]
where $E$ is a set of events and $\rightarrow \subseteq E \times E$ is a strict
partial order representing causal precedence.
\end{definition}

The order $\rightarrow$ expresses only irreducible precedence constraints: if
$e \rightarrow e'$, then $e$ must be known to have occurred before $e'$.
No other ordering is assumed.
In particular, histories do \emph{not} impose a total order, a notion of time, or
an execution schedule.

We write $\Hist$ for the class of histories under consideration.
This choice is parametric and deliberately abstract: histories may represent
database updates, transaction invocations, message deliveries, or any other
events that constrain semantic interpretation.

\begin{definition}[History Extension]
For histories $H_1=(E_1,\rightarrow_1)$ and $H_2=(E_2,\rightarrow_2)$, we write
$H_1 \sqsubseteq H_2$ if $E_1 \subseteq E_2$ and $\rightarrow_1$ is the restriction
of $\rightarrow_2$ to $E_1$.
\end{definition}

Intuitively, $H_2$ extends $H_1$ by revealing additional causally later events.
We interpret $\sqsubseteq$ as information growth, not as time or execution.

\subsection{Outcomes and Specifications}

\begin{definition}[Outcome]
An \emph{outcome} is an element of a set $O$ of fully specified semantic objects.
\end{definition}

Outcomes represent what an observer may conclude once all semantic choices have
been fixed.
Examples include a database state, a serial execution of transactions, a model of
a logic program, or a completed task result.

Importantly, the domain $O$ itself may contain structured or multi-valued objects,
such as probability distributions, sets, or relations.
Ambiguity in our framework does \emph{not} arise from such internal structure.
Rather, ambiguity arises when a history admits \emph{multiple distinct outcomes}
in $O$.

\begin{definition}[Specification]
A \emph{specification} is a function
\[
  \Spec : \Hist \to 2^{O},
\]
mapping each history $H \in \Hist$ to a (possibly empty) set of admissible outcomes.
\end{definition}

The set $\Spec(H)$ captures all outcomes consistent with the evidence in $H$.
When $|\Spec(H)| > 1$, the specification admits multiple mutually incompatible
meanings.
When $|\Spec(H)| = 1$, the meaning of $H$ is fully determined.

Ambiguity in this sense is common and intentional.
Specifications for Datalog with negation, transactional histories, distributed
executions, and probabilistic databases all admit multiple admissible outcomes for
the same history.
In such settings, semantic questions cannot be posed until ambiguity is
constrained.

\subsection{Semantic Refinement}

Ambiguity is constrained by making additional semantic commitments.
We model such commitments abstractly as refinement operators that restrict the
set of admissible outcomes while preserving feasibility.

\begin{definition}[Refinement Operator]
A \emph{refinement operator} is a function
\[
  \varphi : \Hist \times 2^{O} \to 2^{O}
\]
such that for all histories $H$ and outcome sets $S \subseteq O$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item (\emph{Shrinkage}) $\varphi(H,S) \subseteq S$, and
  \item (\emph{Feasibility}) if $S \neq \emptyset$, then $\varphi(H,S) \neq \emptyset$.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

A refinement operator restricts admissible outcomes without introducing new ones
or rendering the specification infeasible.
Operators may be \emph{ambiguity-sensitive}: their effect may depend on the entire
admissible set $S$, not merely on individual outcomes.

\begin{definition}[Refinement by Conditioning]
Given a specification $\Spec$ and a refinement operator $\varphi$, the
\emph{refinement} of $\Spec$ by $\varphi$ is the specification
\[
  (\Spec \mid \varphi)(H) \triangleq \varphi(H,\Spec(H)).
\]
\end{definition}

Refinement acts pointwise on histories but may depend on the current set of
admissible outcomes.
Sequential refinement corresponds to conditioning on multiple semantic
commitments.

\subsection{Interpretations}

Individual refinement operators typically express only partial semantic
commitments.
We therefore model semantic resolution using finite conjunctions of refinement
operators, interpreted as sequential conditioning.

\begin{definition}[Interpretation]
An \emph{interpretation} is a finite conjunction
\[
  I = \varphi_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \varphi_m
\]
of refinement operators, where $\wedge$ denotes sequential refinement.
\end{definition}

Refinement by an interpretation is defined by sequential application:
\[
  \Spec \mid I
  \;\triangleq\;
  (\cdots((\Spec \mid \varphi_1)\mid \varphi_2)\cdots)\mid \varphi_m .
\]

Because refinement operators may be ambiguity-sensitive, the order of conjuncts
can matter.
This non-commutativity is the source of semantic dependency studied in later
sections.

\begin{definition}[Resolved Specification and Resolving Interpretation]
A specification $\Spec$ is \emph{resolved} if for every history $H$,
$|\Spec(H)| = 1$.
An interpretation $I$ \emph{resolves} $\Spec$ if $\Spec \mid I$ is resolved.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Commutation and Dependency}

Each refinement operator $\varphi$ induces a transformation
$\Spec \mapsto (\Spec \mid \varphi)$ on specifications.
Because operators may inspect the current admissible outcome set, the order in
which they are applied can matter.

\begin{definition}[Commutation]
Two refinement operators $\varphi$ and $\psi$ \emph{commute with respect to}
a specification $\Spec$ if
\[
  (\Spec \mid \varphi) \mid \psi
  \;=\;
  (\Spec \mid \psi) \mid \varphi .
\]
\end{definition}

When operators commute, their semantic effect is order-independent.
When they do not commute, one operator may restrict or enable the applicability
of the other.
Such non-commutation exposes irreducible semantic dependency among commitments.

These dependencies are purely semantic.
They make no reference to time, execution order, or mechanism.
Their only role is to determine which commitments can be imposed independently
and which must be staged.

These notions of refinement, conditioning, and commutation provide the raw
semantic substrate on which we build algebraic structure in the next section.

\section{Interpretations as a Conditional Semiring}
\label{sec:conditional-semiring}

This section reframes the interpretation framework of our earlier work as an
algebraic structure suitable for provenance.
The key observation is that interpretations induce a two-level algebra:
before full resolution, provenance expressions live in a
\emph{conditional semiring};
after resolution, this structure collapses to an ordinary semiring.

We emphasize that this algebra is not an arbitrary design choice.
It arises directly from the semantic structure of interpretation:
interpretive predicates compose conjunctively,
while internal case distinctions are expressed disjunctively.
No additional algebraic assumptions are imposed.

\subsection{Interpretations Revisited}

We briefly recall the core constructs from the interpretation framework
introduced in our earlier work, which we treat here as given.

A specification $\Spec : \Hist \to 2^O$ assigns to each history a set of
admissible outcomes.
Interpretation proceeds by refinement: progressively restricting these sets
while preserving feasibility.

An \emph{interpretive predicate} is a refinement operator
\[
  \varphi : \Hist \times 2^O \to 2^O
\]
that shrinks the admissible outcome set while never eliminating all outcomes.
Interpretive predicates may be ambiguity-sensitive: their effect may depend on
the entire current admissible set, not just on individual outcomes.

An \emph{interpretation} is a finite conjunction
$I = \varphi_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \varphi_m$,
interpreted as sequential refinement:
\[
  \Spec \mid I
  \;\triangleq\;
  (\cdots((\Spec \mid \varphi_1)\mid \varphi_2)\cdots)\mid \varphi_m .
\]

An interpretation is \emph{resolving} if $\Spec \mid I$ is single-valued for every
history.
Earlier sections defined \emph{interpretation cost} and \emph{interpretation depth}
as measures of how much semantic commitment is required to achieve such
resolution.

In this paper, we are concerned not with the complexity measures themselves,
but with the algebraic structure induced by interpretations \emph{prior} to full
resolution.

\subsection{Why Interpretation Is Inherently Conjunctive}

A crucial semantic fact underlies everything that follows:

\begin{quote}
\emph{Interpretation proceeds by accumulating commitments.}
\end{quote}

Each interpretive predicate restricts the admissible outcomes.
Applying multiple predicates corresponds to making multiple commitments, all of
which must hold simultaneously.
For this reason, interpretations compose by conjunction.
There is no meaningful notion of ``choosing between'' interpretations within the
semantics of refinement.

Disjunction appears only \emph{within} interpretive predicates, where it expresses
case distinctions:
for example, selecting one outcome if some condition holds, and another otherwise.
Such internal disjunction does not introduce semantic dependency between
predicates and does not contribute to interpretation depth.

This asymmetry is fundamental:
\begin{itemize}
  \item conjunction expresses \emph{semantic commitment} and carries dependency;
  \item disjunction expresses \emph{cases} and is dependency-neutral.
\end{itemize}

As a result, all irreducible semantic dependency lives in the conjunctive
structure of interpretations.

\subsection{Conditional Semiring Structure}

This semantic asymmetry induces a natural algebraic structure.

Consider provenance expressions that track not only how outcomes are derived,
but \emph{under which semantic commitments they are valid}.
Such expressions combine information in two ways:
\begin{itemize}
  \item disjunctively, to account for alternative derivations or cases;
  \item conjunctively, to record accumulated interpretive commitments.
\end{itemize}

However, conjunction is \emph{conditional}:
later commitments may only be meaningful once earlier commitments have been made.
This precisely mirrors the dependency structure captured by interpretation depth.

Formally, this yields a \emph{conditional semiring}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item addition ($+$) is commutative and idempotent, representing disjunction of
        alternatives;
  \item multiplication ($\cdot$) represents conjunction of commitments, but is
        only partially commutative: factors commute exactly when the corresponding
        interpretive predicates commute.
\end{itemize}

Each commuting layer of an interpretation induces a semiring fragment.
Layers compose sequentially, yielding a \emph{stack of semirings} indexed by
semantic dependency.
Provenance expressions are therefore evaluated conditionally, with each layer
conditioning on the certificates produced by earlier layers.

This algebra is not imposed by fiat.
It is the algebraic reflection of the interpretation framework’s semantics.


\subsection{Collapse to Semiring Provenance After Resolution}

Once an interpretation fully resolves the specification, ambiguity disappears.
All conditioning predicates are discharged, and no further semantic commitments
remain.

At this point, the conditional semiring collapses to an ordinary commutative
semiring.
Provenance expressions no longer depend on interpretive certificates and reduce
to standard semiring annotations.

Thus, classical algebraic provenance appears not as a primitive semantic object,
but as the \emph{endpoint} of interpretation.
This explains both its power and its limitations:
it applies precisely when semantic ambiguity has been eliminated.


\paragraph{Depth-0 specifications.}
Some specifications are intrinsically resolved: for every history, they admit a
single admissible outcome, or can be resolved without staging any semantic
commitments.
For such specifications, interpretation depth is zero.
No conditioning is required, and the conditional structure introduced above
collapses to a single semiring.

In this case, interpretation provenance coincides exactly with classical
semiring-based provenance.
Traditional provenance methods therefore characterize the depth-0 fragment of
our framework: semantic settings in which ambiguity has already been eliminated,
and no interpretive commitments are required to obtain algebraic structure.

\subsection{Summary}

Interpretations induce an algebra with two distinct regimes:
\begin{itemize}
  \item before resolution, provenance lives in a conditional semiring structured
        by semantic dependency;
  \item after resolution, this structure collapses to classical semiring
        provenance.
\end{itemize}

This perspective unifies the interpretation framework with algebraic provenance
and clarifies why provenance succeeds only after semantic commitments have been
made.
Later sections instantiate this structure concretely for Datalog with negation
and transactional semantics.

\section{Interpretations and Quotient Engineering}
\label{sec:interpretations}
% ================================================================

Sections~\ref{sec:prelim} and~\ref{sec:conditional-semiring} established the
semantic and algebraic structure induced by interpretations.
In this section, we make explicit the \emph{semantic effect} of interpretation and
connect it to the complexity measures introduced in our earlier work.

At a high level, an interpretation acts in two stages.
It first restricts the space of admissible outcomes to those compatible with its
semantic commitments, and then collapses distinctions among the remaining
outcomes.
Together, these steps induce a quotient on the outcome space.
This restriction--identification process provides the semantic bridge between
ambiguity and algebra, and explains why algebraic provenance becomes applicable
only after sufficient resolution.

\subsection{Restriction of Admissible Outcomes}

Recall that a specification $\Spec$ assigns to each history $H$ a set
$\Spec(H) \subseteq O$ of admissible outcomes.
An interpretation $I$ refines $\Spec$ by sequential conditioning:
\[
  \Spec \mid I \;:\; H \mapsto (\Spec \mid I)(H).
\]

The primary semantic effect of refinement is restriction.
Outcomes incompatible with the commitments expressed by $I$ are eliminated and
play no further semantic role.
All subsequent reasoning is carried out solely over the restricted set
$(\Spec \mid I)(H)$.

This restriction reflects the meaning of interpretation.
Semantic commitments do not reinterpret incompatible outcomes, nor do they merge
them with admissible ones.
They simply rule them out.

\subsection{Identification of Remaining Outcomes}

Restriction alone does not exhaust the effect of interpretation.
Even after incompatible outcomes have been eliminated, multiple admissible
outcomes may remain.
An interpretation may nevertheless render these outcomes semantically
indistinguishable.

Fix a specification $\Spec$, an interpretation $I$, and a history $H$, and write
\[
  O_H^I \;\triangleq\; (\Spec \mid I)(H)
\]
for the restricted outcome set.
We say that two outcomes $o,o' \in O_H^I$ are \emph{equivalent under $I$ at $H$},
written $o \sim_{I,H} o'$, if no refinement operator appearing in $I$ ever
distinguishes between them at $H$.
Intuitively, $I$ never depends on which of these outcomes holds.

\begin{definition}[Interpretation-Induced Quotient]
For each history $H$, an interpretation $I$ induces a quotient
\[
  O_H^I \big/ {\sim_{I,H}},
\]
obtained by identifying outcomes in $O_H^I$ that are equivalent under $I$.
\end{definition}

The equivalence relation $\sim_{I,H}$ is total on the restricted domain $O_H^I$.
Outcomes ruled out by the interpretation are simply absent from the quotient.
Both restriction and identification are therefore intrinsic to the conditioning
semantics of interpretation.

\begin{remark}[History-indexed quotients]
Because refinement operators may depend on the history and on intermediate
admissible sets, the equivalence relation $\sim_{I,H}$ may vary with $H$.
Interpretation thus induces a family of quotients indexed by histories, rather
than a single global quotient on $O$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Resolution as a Limiting Case}

When an interpretation $I$ resolves $\Spec$, the restricted set
$(\Spec \mid I)(H)$ is a singleton for every history $H$.
In this case, no nontrivial identification remains: each history admits exactly
one equivalence class.

Resolution is therefore the limiting case of quotient construction.
It is not a distinct semantic operation, but the endpoint of the same process of
restriction and identification under increasingly strong commitments.

\subsection{Equivalence, Cost, and Depth}

Because interpretations act only through the quotients they induce, many distinct
interpretations may be semantically equivalent.

Recall that two interpretations $I$ and $J$ are equivalent for $\Spec$ if
\[
  \Spec \mid I \;=\; \Spec \mid J .
\]
Equivalent interpretations induce the same restriction and the same quotient, and
therefore support identical downstream semantic reasoning and provenance.

Within an equivalence class, interpretations may nevertheless differ in their
structure.
Some may assert redundant commitments; others may impose unnecessary sequencing
dependencies.
This motivates the complexity measures introduced in our earlier work.

Interpretation \emph{cost} measures the volume of semantic commitment required to
induce a quotient: the number of refinement operators applied.
Interpretation \emph{depth} measures irreducible dependency structure: the minimum
number of semantic stages required to impose those commitments.

Cost-minimal and depth-minimal interpretations therefore correspond to
\emph{parsimonious quotient constructions}.
They induce the same quotient with fewer commitments or with fewer unavoidable
dependency stages.
From the quotient perspective, cost and depth are not abstract measures: they
quantify the structural effort required to collapse semantic distinctions.

\subsection{Quotient Engineering}

Quotients live in the outcome space: they determine which semantic distinctions
among admissible outcomes are preserved and which are collapsed.
However, quotients are not selected directly.
They are induced by upstream semantic choices—specifications, notions of
observation, and the interpretive predicates used to resolve ambiguity.

A specification $\Spec$, together with a notion of observation, determines which
outcomes are distinguishable in principle.
An interpretation then enforces additional commitments that restrict admissible
outcomes and identify remaining distinctions.
The combination of $\Spec$ and interpretation therefore induces a quotient on the
outcome space.
Different choices upstream may induce different quotients even over the same
histories and outcomes.

In many domains, there is no uniquely correct quotient.
For example, well-founded and stable semantics for Datalog with negation induce
different quotients, each defensible on semantic grounds.
The interpretation framework does not prescribe a single choice; instead, it
exposes the space of admissible ones by making semantic commitments explicit.

Semantic choices have structural consequences.
Each induced quotient determines the structure of the associated conditional
semiring, whose dependency structure governs the cost and latency of provenance.
Coarser quotients may admit simpler algebraic structure, while finer quotients may
require deeper or more dependent interpretations.

We refer to the practice of selecting interpretive predicates and interpretations
with an eye toward the induced outcome quotient as \emph{quotient engineering}.
Although the design actions occur in the specification and interpretation domains,
the effects are best understood in terms of the resulting quotient.
Quotient engineering is therefore a joint semantic and structural design problem,
balancing fidelity of meaning against algebraic complexity.

This perspective applies uniformly across domains.
In Datalog with negation, different interpretations induce stratified,
well-founded, or stable quotients.
In transactional systems, they correspond to different visibility and ordering
assumptions.
In probabilistic settings, they correspond to different conditioning and
independence assumptions.

\subsection{Summary}

Interpretations act by first restricting outcome spaces and then identifying
remaining outcomes according to explicit semantic commitments.
The resulting quotients make ambiguity and resolution first-class semantic
objects.

Cost and depth characterize the effort required to induce a quotient, while
quotient engineering frames the semantic and structural tradeoffs involved in
choosing among them.
Viewed this way, interpretation is not an auxiliary mechanism but the semantic
substrate on which provenance rests.

Later sections instantiate this perspective concretely for Datalog with negation
and transactional semantics.

\section{Layer Certificates and Interpretation Provenance}
\label{sec:certificates}
% ================================================================

Sections~\ref{sec:conditional-semiring} and~\ref{sec:interpretations} established
the semantic and algebraic structure induced by interpretations.
In this section, we address a complementary question:

\begin{quote}
\emph{What information must be retained in order to support interpretation?}
\end{quote}

Interpretations act by conditioning and quotienting outcome spaces.
However, downstream semantic reasoning does not require access to the full history
of semantic commitments.
Instead, it suffices to retain summaries of prior resolution that determine which
distinctions remain relevant.
We formalize this idea using \emph{layer certificates}.

Layer certificates are not new semantic objects.
They are representations of \emph{sufficient information} for interpretation:
enough to support further conditioning, but potentially much smaller than the
interpretation itself.
They provide a principled interface between semantic resolution and provenance.

\subsection{Certificates as Sufficient Information}

Fix a specification $\Spec$ and an interpretation $I$ with a chosen layering
$L_1,\ldots,L_k$ as in Section~\ref{sec:conditional-semiring}.
Each layer imposes semantic commitments that restrict and identify outcomes,
producing a refined specification
\[
  \Spec_i \;\triangleq\; \Spec \mid (\varphi_0 \wedge \cdots \wedge \varphi_i).
\]

Our goal is to characterize what information about $\Spec_i$ must be retained in
order to support subsequent interpretation.

\paragraph{Certificates for individual refinements.}
We begin by defining what it means for a value to summarize the effect of a single
refinement operator.

\begin{definition}[Certificate for a refinement operator]
Let $\varphi$ be a refinement operator and let $C$ be a value.
We write $\llbracket C \rrbracket \subseteq O$ for the set of outcomes consistent
with $C$ (its \emph{denotation}).
We say that $C$ is a \emph{certificate} for $\varphi$, written $\varphi \models C$,
if for all histories $H$ and all nonempty outcome sets $S \subseteq O$,
\[
  \varphi(H,S) \;\subseteq\; S \cap \llbracket C \rrbracket .
\]
\end{definition}

Intuitively, $\varphi \models C$ means that conditioning on $\varphi$ never admits
outcomes outside $\llbracket C \rrbracket$.
The certificate $C$ may be weaker than $\varphi$ itself; it need only rule out
outcomes that $\varphi$ would rule out.

\paragraph{Sufficient certificates for continuation.}
We now lift this notion from individual refinements to suffixes of an
interpretation.

\begin{definition}[Sufficient certificate for a continuation]
Fix a specification $\Spec$ and an interpretation
$I = \varphi_0 \wedge \cdots \wedge \varphi_k$.
Let
\[
  \Spec_i \;\triangleq\; \Spec \mid (\varphi_0 \wedge \cdots \wedge \varphi_i)
  \quad\text{and}\quad
  J \;\triangleq\; \varphi_{i+1} \wedge \cdots \wedge \varphi_k .
\]
A certificate $C_i$ is \emph{sufficient for the continuation $J$} if, for all
histories $H$,
\[
  (\Spec_i \mid J)(H)
  \;=\;
  \bigl( (\Spec \mid \chi_{C_i}) \mid J \bigr)(H),
\]
where $\chi_{C_i}(H,S) \triangleq S \cap \llbracket C_i \rrbracket$.
\end{definition}

That is, replacing the refined specification $\Spec_i$ by conditioning on the
certificate $C_i$ has no effect on the outcome of any subsequent interpretation.
All semantic distinctions relevant to $J$ are preserved by $C_i$.

\paragraph{Layer certificates.}
A \emph{layer certificate} is simply a certificate that is sufficient for all
subsequent layers of an interpretation.

\begin{definition}[Layer Certificate]
Given a layering $L_1,\ldots,L_k$, a \emph{layer certificate} for layer $i$ is a
certificate $C_i$ that is sufficient for the continuation
$\varphi_{i+1} \wedge \cdots \wedge \varphi_k$.
\end{definition}

Intuitively, a layer certificate is a sufficient statistic for interpretation
beyond layer $i$.
It need not encode the full refined specification or the full interpretation so
far; it need only preserve the distinctions that matter for future semantic
commitments.

\paragraph{Interpretation provenance.}
Certificates mediate between layers of the conditional semiring.
Rather than conditioning later layers on the entire refined specification,
interpretation proceeds by conditioning on certificates produced by earlier
layers.

This separation cleanly factors interpretation provenance into:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the algebraic structure induced by interpretation
        (Section~\ref{sec:conditional-semiring}), and
  \item the information carried between layers, as captured by certificates.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Certificates, Cost, and Depth}

Layer certificates interact with, but are distinct from, interpretation cost and
depth.

Cost and depth measure the \emph{structure of semantic commitment}:
how many commitments are required, and how they depend on one another.
Certificates describe the \emph{information interface} between those commitments.

In particular:
\begin{itemize}
  \item interpretation depth determines how many layers of certificates are
        required;
  \item certificates determine what information must be preserved between layers.
\end{itemize}

These concerns are deliberately separated.
A shallow interpretation may still require rich certificates, while a deep
interpretation may admit compact certificates if each layer collapses many
distinctions.

\paragraph{Information carried by certificates.}
Layer certificates summarize the effect of earlier semantic commitments, but they
cannot be arbitrarily small.
What information a certificate must retain is constrained by the expressive power
of the remaining interpretation.

In particular, if later refinement operators can test or branch on distinctions
introduced earlier, then certificates must preserve enough information to support
those distinctions.
This yields unavoidable lower bounds on certificate size that are semantic rather
than representational.

We make this concrete in Section~\ref{sec:datalog}, where stratified negation
provides a sharp illustration: each negation stratum induces an interface whose
possible valuations must be distinguished by any certificate sufficient for
subsequent strata.
As a result, the information content of certificates is bounded below by the
semantic variability exposed at stratum boundaries.

\subsection{Summary}

Layer certificates provide a principled interface between semantic resolution and
provenance.
They summarize the effect of interpretation layers in a form sufficient for
further conditioning, without exposing unnecessary detail.

By separating interpretation structure from informational representation, the
framework supports provenance that is both semantically grounded and flexible.
Later sections show how concrete domains instantiate these ideas and exploit this
separation.
