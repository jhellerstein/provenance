I read the full main.tex source: 3,083 lines, including the appendices. My candid assessment is:

Current acceptance odds at PODS: low-to-moderate, roughly 25–35%.
With a focused revision and tightened formal core: plausibly 45–55%.
The idea is strong and venue-relevant, but the draft currently risks rejection for overbreadth, underformalized central claims, and several definitions/theorems that reviewers may view as false or at least not justified as stated.

Overall judgment

The paper has a real contribution: provenance should explain not only derivations under a fixed semantics, but also the semantic commitments that made the semantics determinate. That is a good PODS idea. It connects provenance, possible worlds, negation, repairs, transaction isolation, and explanation in a way that feels fresh.

But the present draft is trying to be:

1. a general theory of semantic commitments,
2. a provenance semiring paper,
3. a transaction/isolation paper,
4. a Datalog-with-negation paper,
5. a Shapley/responsibility complexity paper,
6. a systems-lineage vision paper.

That is too much for PODS unless the formal center is rock solid. Right now, the paper’s center is not quite stable enough: the definitions of commitments, persistence, determinations, filtration, and resolution are doing a lot of work, but several claims depend on assumptions that are not stated, and some examples do not faithfully instantiate the formal definitions.

My strongest advice: make this a paper about determination provenance as a refinement of possible-world/provenance semantics, with the filtration as the main new algebraic object. Treat transactions and Datalog as two carefully worked instantiations, but reduce the systems-lineage material to motivation or a short discussion.

The highest-priority issues

1. The central theorem “resolution is necessary and sufficient for provenance” is too strong

Theorem 2.1 currently says classical semiring provenance over an ambiguous specification is well-defined iff the specification is determined.

That will draw fire. A reviewer can object immediately: possible-worlds provenance, probabilistic provenance, incomplete databases, and semiring annotations over alternatives already allow forms of provenance without a single determined outcome. Your own determination provenance is a function from determinations to provenance values, which is provenance before choosing a single determination.

The theorem is trying to say something more specific: ordinary single-instance positive semiring provenance does not explain mutually incompatible admissible outcomes without externalizing the choice of world/model/schedule. That is true and important, but the current wording overclaims.

I would replace the theorem with something like:

A single classical K-relation is sound and complete for a specification only when all admissible outcomes agree on tuple membership and derivational annotations. In general, ambiguous specifications require provenance indexed by resolutions/determinations.

That gives you a cleaner bridge to \(P_\Spec(t):\mathcal D\to K\), and avoids the false impression that “provenance is impossible before resolution.”

2. The definition of “commitment” conflicts with later “sealing” commitments

Definition 2.4 requires a commitment to produce a nonempty proper subset:
\[
\Spec(H\cdot \varphi)\subsetneq \Spec(H).
\]

But the persistence-canonicalization appendix later says sealing commitments can be non-filtering at a completed history:
\[
\Spec(H\cdot \varphi_{\seal(S)})=\Spec(H).
\]

So either sealing is not a commitment, or the definition of commitment must allow no-op commitments in retrospect. This is not a minor issue: sealing is one of the paper’s conceptual pillars.

I would split the vocabulary:

* commitment event: an irrevocable semantic assertion, possibly non-filtering at a particular history;
* filtering commitment: a commitment whose immediate effect strictly reduces \(\Spec(H)\);
* seal: a commitment that may be non-filtering retrospectively but makes future entailment persistent.

Then “minimal resolving determinations” should probably eliminate redundant no-op commitments unless they are needed to justify later entailments.

3. The determination semiring itself is elementary; the filtration is the real contribution

The support semiring
(2^{\mathcal D},\cup,\cap,\emptyset,\mathcal D)
is just the Boolean semiring of possible-world supports. That is fine, but it is not surprising enough by itself. The paper currently sometimes presents “supports form a semiring” as a major contribution; a PODS reviewer may say, “yes, of course.”

The novelty is instead:

The possible worlds are not flat; they are generated by layered, partially commuting commitments, and this induces a filtration that query evaluation respects.

That should become the main algebraic theorem. I would downshift the rhetoric around the Boolean support semiring and upshift the filtration, query-relative depth, and preservation story.

4. The filtration needs a more rigorous canonicalization story

The body says layerings are “maximal commuting batches” analogous to Foata normal form, and that agreement at level k is well-defined. But the appendix later lists “dynamic Foata normal form” as an open question.

That is dangerous: the paper depends on a canonical layered form, while the appendix says canonical layered form is open.

You need one of these fixes:

1. Restrict the main theorem to commitment bases with a given stratification/layering function. Then the filtration is defined relative to that layering.
2. Define determinations modulo a fixed trace monoid where independence is static.
3. Move dynamic commutativity out of the main theory and use it only as intuition.

For PODS, I would choose option 1: define a layered commitment basis as part of the input. Then transactions/Datalog instantiate it. You can leave dynamic Foata normalization as future work.

5. The examples do not always enumerate the actual \mathcal D

The intro example says \mathcal D=\{D_{\mathsf{in}},D_{\mathsf{out}}\}, but with three concurrent relevant actions T_2,T_3,T_Q, there are more serial orders unless you quotient them by query-relevant equivalence. Later the appendix uses a different transactional example with five transactions and different initial contents.

This is fixable, but important. Since robustness and responsibility quantify over \mathcal D, you must be precise about whether \mathcal D is:

* all resolving determinations,
* minimal resolving determinations,
* quotient classes of determinations under outcome equivalence,
* or query-relative certificates.

Right now it slides between these. I would introduce an explicit quotient/certificate abstraction early, or make the example small enough that all determinations can be listed.

For the intro, I would use the smallest possible example:

T_I:\textsf{ins }S(2,d),\quad
T_D:\textsf{del }S(2,d),\quad
T_Q:Q(y)\leftarrow S(k,y)

Then explicitly list all serializations and group them into “d-visible” and “d-absent” supports. Do not pretend there are only two determinations unless the basis really makes that true.

6. The transaction section is currently the weakest formal instantiation

The transaction material is intuitive, but it will be attacked.

Specific issues:

* “Under read committed, the specification is already determined” is questionable unless the history already includes the read values. RC can admit many observable outcomes under different interleavings.
* The conflict graph definition removes edges adjacent to committed transactions. That is plausible as an active enforcement graph, but not the classical serialization graph used to define serializability.
* “Serializability with commit/abort basis has depth 1 when the conflict graph is acyclic” does not resolve all ambiguity if different topological orders can yield different observations.
* Ordering commitments are introduced as an “enriched basis,” but the examples rely on them heavily. The body should decide whether the main transactional basis is commit/abort, ordering, or both.

I would either substantially tighten this section or demote it. The cleanest path is:

* define the transactional specification in terms of admissible serial orders;
* define ordering commitments as the primary commitment basis;
* treat commit/abort as an optimization/enforcement detail;
* prove only a modest claim: acyclic conflict constraints yield a single-layer determination basis; cycles/validation dependencies induce multi-layer commitments.

Do not overstate isolation-level depth unless you can prove it cleanly.

7. The Datalog section is promising but overclaims in places

This is probably the best formal application, but it needs sharper boundaries.

Concerns:

* The theorem relating WFS to supports across stable models is not true in full generality as stated unless stable models exist and the class is restricted carefully. WFS is not simply skeptical stable semantics for all normal programs.
* The theorem says “finite normal Datalog” in the monus-elimination statement, but monus provenance is really a stratified-negation tool; for unstratified negation, the comparison needs a separate formulation.
* The monus-elimination theorem states “for any naturally ordered semiring K,” but the proof uses zero-divisor-freeness. The theorem statement must include the actual algebraic hypotheses.
* “For programs with unstratified negation, monus is undefined while determination provenance remains well-defined across stable models” needs caveats: stable models may not exist, and stable-model provenance may be empty/inconsistent depending on the program.

I think the Datalog material could become a strong PODS contribution if you narrow it:

For stratified negation, sealing commitments recover monus-style support. For a restricted class of unstratified programs with independent negative SCCs and stable models, the filtration separates stratified, WFS-like, and stable-model readings.

That is much safer than claiming a general unification of all negation semantics.

8. Responsibility is interesting but feels like a second paper

The Shapley responsibility section is intellectually appealing, but it may dilute the main paper. It introduces a new complexity theorem, a new game, and a new tractability frontier. That is a lot after the reader has already absorbed determinations, filtrations, transactions, and negation.

For PODS, I would keep responsibility but shrink it to:

* definition,
* one example,
* one theorem: #P-hardness,
* one tractability theorem, perhaps in appendix.

Frame it as evidence that the algebraic structure buys quantitative diagnosis, not as a coequal contribution.

Concrete revision plan

Make the abstract narrower and sharper

Current abstract promises too much: transactions, Datalog semantics, monus, Shapley, tractability. I would rewrite around one spine:

1. ambiguous specifications have many admissible outcomes;
2. determinations resolve ambiguity via commitments;
3. provenance indexed by determinations yields supports;
4. layered commitments induce a filtration;
5. positive query evaluation respects the filtration;
6. applications: negation and transactions.

Move Shapley to the final abstract sentence, or omit from abstract entirely.

Strengthen the formal setup before examples

Add a subsection after specifications:

“Standing assumptions.”

State explicitly:

* finite outcome/admissible sets where needed;
* finite commitment bases;
* whether \mathcal D contains all minimal determinations or quotient classes;
* whether determinations are sequences, traces modulo commutation, or layered objects;
* whether a commitment may be non-filtering;
* whether histories include commitment events or commitments live outside histories.

Right now these assumptions are scattered or implicit.

Replace Theorem 2.1

Do not say provenance iff determined. Say something like:

Theorem: Classical provenance is pointwise in determinations.
For each resolving determination D, ordinary K-provenance is well-defined for \(\Spec\mid D\). Conversely, any provenance representation that is sound and complete for all admissible outcomes must distinguish determinations whenever two determinations disagree on tuple membership or annotation.

That gives you the same conceptual payoff without an indefensible impossibility claim.

Make filtration the main theorem

The paper should build toward:

Theorem: Let \mathcal D be a finite set of resolving determinations equipped with a layered equivalence sequence \equiv_0,\ldots,\equiv_d. Then supports form a filtered idempotent semiring, and positive relational algebra is filtration-nonincreasing.

That is clean, true, and central.

Then Datalog and transactions instantiate the layered equivalence sequence.

Repair the running example

Use one running example throughout the paper. At present, the intro has initial \{S(0,b)\} plus T_1,T_2,T_3,T_Q; the appendix switches to an initially empty database with five transactions. That looks accidental.

Pick one. Make it tiny. List the determinations/supports explicitly. Then reuse the same notation everywhere.

Recast transactions as an application, not a theorem-heavy pillar

I would avoid claiming full depth characterizations for RC/SER/SI unless you are prepared to prove them. Instead:

* define ordering determinations;
* show how robust/contingent query answers arise;
* give one proposition about acyclic conflict constraints producing single-layer supports;
* give one example where overlapping conflicts produce depth >1.

That is enough.

Tighten Datalog theorem statements

For the main body, state a restricted theorem only. Put the general nested SCC story in the appendix with careful caveats.

For monus elimination, fix the theorem statement:

* stratified programs;
* naturally ordered, zero-divisor-free semiring, or restrict to Boolean/support equivalence;
* distinguish support equivalence from full provenance equivalence.

Cut or compress appendices

The appendices are rich but sprawling. For submission, I would keep:

1. formal proof details for filtration and support algebra;
2. one Datalog worked example;
3. one transaction worked example;
4. responsibility proofs.

I would either remove or heavily shorten:

* protocol-specific 2PL/MVTO discussion;
* systems-lineage appendix;
* open questions;
* broad certificates discussion.

Those are good future-paper/blog/vision material, but they weaken the PODS submission by making the theory look less finished.

Line-level/formal red flags to fix

A non-exhaustive list:

* \acmConference[Conference]{Full Conference Name}{Month Year}{Location} must be cleaned before submission.
* The source cites anon2026coordinationcriterion and hellerstein2026complexity; make sure double-blind rules and anonymization are handled correctly.
* Definition 2.4 says commitments strictly shrink \(\Spec(H)\); persistence canonicalization later uses non-filtering seals.
* Definition 2.6 persistence should probably refer to the exclusion set
\[
\mathrm{Excl}_\varphi(H)=\Spec(H)\setminus \Spec(H\cdot\varphi)
\]
    rather than all o\in O, otherwise it behaves oddly for outcomes not admissible at H.
* “This set is finite” for \mathcal D requires finite \(\Spec(H)\) or a finite quotient.
* “Each \equiv_k is an equivalence relation” needs a canonical layering assumption.
* “The determination semiring coincides with \mathrm{PosBool}(\Phi)” is only true modulo an encoding of determinations as complete assignments/sets of commitments; state this carefully.
* Proposition “Read committed is already determined” is likely false unless outcomes are defined very narrowly.
* The Datalog outcome order
    \bot \preceq \mathbf u \preceq \{\mathbf t,\mathbf f\}
    should be written more formally; \mathbf t and \mathbf f are incomparable above \mathbf u.
* Monus-elimination theorem says “any naturally ordered semiring,” but proof uses zero-divisor-freeness.
* The stable/WFS/stratified table is good pedagogically, but the text immediately before it has an awkward line break: “only when they resolve everything” is split around the wraptable.
* The related work should engage possible-worlds/incomplete-database provenance more deeply, because reviewers will see that as the closest baseline.

What I would emphasize in the submission

The winning version of this paper is not “we invented another semiring.” It is:

Existing provenance explains why an answer follows once a semantics is fixed. But many database semantics are relational rather than functional: stable models, transaction schedules, repairs, isolation-level outcomes. We introduce provenance for the commitments that select among these admissible meanings. The resulting supports over determinations form a filtered semiring; the filtration measures how much semantic resolution a query actually depends on.

That is crisp, PODS-shaped, and defensible.

Bottom line

I would not submit the draft in its current form unless the deadline leaves no alternative. The idea is good enough that it deserves a cleaner shot.

The minimum viable revision is:

1. weaken/rewrite the “resolution iff provenance” theorem;
2. fix commitment/seal consistency;
3. make layered determinations a formal input or prove canonical layering;
4. clean up the running example and \mathcal D;
5. narrow the transaction and Datalog claims;
6. make filtration the main theorem;
7. cut speculative appendices.

Do those, and the paper becomes a serious PODS contender rather than a fascinating but vulnerable manifesto.
